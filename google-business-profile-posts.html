Google Business Profile Posts That Bring Customers

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

Most small businesses set up a Google Business Profile and then never touch it again — and that is exactly what their competitors are counting on. The profile is often the first thing a local searcher sees, and a profile with no recent posts looks like a business that may have closed, changed hands, or stopped caring. A post is the simplest way to change that impression: one short update, with one clear photo and one next step, tells a searcher the business is open, active and ready to help. This guide covers the four kinds of post worth writing — updates, offers, events and product posts — how to write each one, what photo to use, how often to post, and how to measure whether the posts are working. Nothing here promises a ranking. What posts do, honestly, is help the searchers who already find your profile choose you instead of the business next door. If your profile is not set up yet, start with our Google Business Profile setup guide; if the wider local picture needs work, read our Google Map Pack guide and our local SEO audit checklist alongside this one.

A Google Business Profile post is a short message that appears on your business listing in Google Search and Maps. It is not an advert, and it is not a social media post — it is a note pinned to the front of your shop window, seen by people who are already looking for a business like yours, in your area, right now. That audience is the reason posts matter more than their size suggests. A person who finds your profile in a local search is not browsing idly. They have a problem to solve, a service to book, or a place to visit, and they are comparing the few businesses Google showed them. The profile that answers their question, shows recent work, and makes the next step obvious will win that comparison more often than the profile that only lists a name, an address and a phone number. The post is where that answer lives. It can announce a seasonal offer, show a job finished this week, remind customers of a holiday opening time, or introduce a new service — and each of those gives the searcher a reason to choose this business today rather than to keep scrolling. The work is small: a weekly post takes less time than answering two phone calls. The habit is what most businesses lack, and this guide builds it, post type by post type.

What a Business Profile Post Is — and What It Is Not

A post sits on your profile, below the main business details, and it is seen by people who search for your business or for the kind of business you run in your area. It usually shows a photo, a few lines of text, and a button — call, book, visit, learn more, or a similar next step. Posts are short-lived by design: an update post is typically shown for a limited period before it moves down the profile, which is why posting regularly matters more than writing one perfect post. Think of posts as the noticeboard on a shop front. A noticeboard with a fresh notice tells a passer-by the shop is open and paying attention. A noticeboard with a faded notice from last year tells them the opposite. The same is true of a profile. A post is not a replacement for the profile itself — the name, address, phone number, opening hours, categories and reviews still do the heavy lifting, and a business without those basics should fix them first, following our setup guide and our reviews guide. A post is also not a social media strategy. It will not build a following, it will not go viral, and it should not be written as if it will. Its audience is small and valuable: people already searching, already nearby, already deciding. Write for them, and only for them.

The Four Post Types Worth Using

Google Business Profile offers several post types, and most businesses only need four of them. The first is the update, sometimes called a "What's new" post: a short piece of news about the business — a new service, a finished job, a seasonal reminder, a change to opening hours, a team introduction. Updates are the everyday workhorse of posting, because a business always has some news, even if it feels too small to mention. The second is the offer: a promotion with a clear benefit, a start and end date, and a condition if there is one. Offers work when the benefit is specific — a named service, a named saving, a named date — and they fail when they are vague, because a vague offer gives the searcher nothing to act on. The third is the event: something happening on a specific date, at a specific place — an open day, a workshop, a sale weekend, a community event the business is part of. Events are worth posting because they give a searcher a date to remember and a reason to visit on that date, and because an event post stays relevant until the date passes. The fourth is the product post: a single product or service, with a photo, a short description and a price or a price range if you choose to show one. Product posts suit shops and service businesses with a clear offer — a named treatment, a named package, a seasonal product — and they work best when the product is one a searcher would recognise and want. Most weeks, an update is enough. Use offers when there is a real offer, events when there is a real event, and product posts when a product deserves the spotlight. A profile that only ever posts offers looks like a business that is always on sale; a profile that mixes the four looks like a business that is alive.

How to Write a Post That Gets Read

A profile post is read on a phone, in a few seconds, by someone comparing several businesses. Write for that reader. Put the most important words first: the offer, the news or the event, in the first line, in plain words. "Free boiler check this month for homes in Leeds" will be read; "We are delighted to announce that, as part of our ongoing commitment to our valued customers..." will not. Say one thing per post. A post that tries to announce an offer, introduce a new team member and remind customers about holiday hours will say none of them clearly, and the searcher will remember none of them. Give the detail a customer needs to act: the dates, the area covered, what is included, and anything that is excluded. If the offer only applies to a certain service, say so. If the event needs booking, say so. If the update is about a new service, say who it is for. Then end with one next step, and only one: call, book, visit, or send a message. A post with three different buttons and no clear instruction is a post the searcher will close. Keep the whole post short — a few sentences, not a paragraph of background — and put any longer detail on your website, linked from the post, so the searcher who wants more can find it without the post itself becoming a wall of text. Read the post aloud before publishing it. If it sounds like something a real person would say to a customer standing in the shop, it is ready. If it sounds like a press release, rewrite it.

Photos: One Real Photo, Well Chosen

Every post needs a photo, and the photo does more work than the words. A searcher scanning a profile will look at the photo first, and a good photo will make them read the words; a bad photo will make them scroll past. The best photo for a post is a real photo of your own business: the finished job, the product on display, the inside of the shop, the team member the post is about, the event venue as it will look. It should be taken in good light, with the subject filling the frame, and with nothing distracting in the background. Avoid stock photos, because a stock photo of a smiling stranger tells the searcher nothing about your business, and it looks the same as every other business's post. Avoid images full of text, because the words in an image are hard to read at the small size a post appears at on a phone, and because the post already has words — the photo's job is to show, not to say. Check the photo at phone size before publishing: shrink it on your own screen, and if you cannot tell what it shows in one second, choose another. Keep the same look across posts — similar light, similar framing, similar colours — so the profile feels like one business rather than a collection of unrelated images. And make sure the photo matches the post. A post about a finished kitchen should show a kitchen, not a logo. A post about a team member should show that person, not a generic office. A mismatch between photo and words makes the searcher doubt both.

Offers That Bring Customers Without Discounting Everything

Offers are the post type most likely to bring a customer in this week — and the type most likely to damage a business if used badly. An offer works when it is specific, time-limited and honest. Specific means a named service or product, with a named benefit: "a free check", "a named package at a named price", "a booking in a named week". Time-limited means a clear start and end date, so the searcher knows the offer is real and knows when it ends. Honest means the condition is stated plainly: if the offer only applies to new customers, say so; if it only applies to one service, say so; if it cannot be combined with another offer, say so. An offer that hides its condition will bring in customers who feel misled, and a customer who feels misled will not return. Do not make every post an offer. A business that is always discounting teaches its customers to wait for the discount, and it teaches searchers that the normal price is not the real price. Use offers for real reasons: a quiet period you want to fill, a new service you want people to try, a seasonal moment when customers are already buying. Between offers, post updates, events and product posts, so the profile shows a business worth visiting at full price, not only at a discount. And when an offer ends, take the post down or let it expire — an out-of-date offer on a profile tells a searcher the business is not paying attention, which is the opposite of what a post is for.

Events, Updates and Seasonal Posts

Events and seasonal updates are where a profile earns its keep through the year, because they show a business that is part of its community and aware of its customers' calendar. An event post should name the event, the date, the place and the reason to come, in that order, and it should be posted early enough for people to plan — a week or two ahead for a local event, longer for a bigger one. A seasonal update should be timed to the season it belongs to: holiday opening hours before the holiday, not during it; a seasonal service while customers are still deciding, not after they have booked elsewhere. The seasonal posts that work best are the practical ones: opening hours, last booking dates, seasonal services, and reminders about the things customers forget — that a service needs booking ahead, that a product needs ordering, that a shop will be closed on a certain day. Between the big moments, post the small news that shows the business is active: a job finished this week, a product back in stock, a new team member, a thank-you to customers after a busy period. Small news, posted regularly, builds the same impression as big news: this business is open, busy and paying attention. Keep a simple list of ideas — the seasons, the holidays, the recurring events, the services that change through the year — and work through it, so you are never starting a week with a blank page. A profile that posts about the season its customers are living in will always feel more relevant than one that posts the same generic message all year.

How Often to Post — and a Weekly Routine

Once a week is the rhythm that works for most small businesses. It is frequent enough that the profile always shows something recent, and it is a small enough commitment that it survives a busy week. A business with frequent news can post more often — a restaurant with a changing menu, a shop with weekly arrivals, a service business with seasonal peaks — but more posts only help if each one says something worth saying. Two good posts a week beat five empty ones. Set a fixed day for posting, and keep it. A routine that depends on remembering will be forgotten; a routine with a fixed day and a short list of ideas will be kept. The routine itself is simple. Once a month, spend half an hour making a list: the offers coming up, the events, the seasonal dates, the jobs you expect to finish, the products you want to highlight. Each week, pick one item from the list, write three or four sentences, choose one real photo, add one next step, and publish. Then check the profile the next day, to make sure the post appears as intended and the button works. That is the whole routine, and it takes less time than most business owners spend answering two phone calls. If a week is genuinely too busy, post a short update rather than nothing — a photo of the week's work and one sentence about it is enough to keep the profile looking alive. Silence for two months will undo the work of the weeks before it, because the profile will again look like a business that has stopped paying attention.

Calls to Action: Give Every Post a Next Step

A post without a next step is a message with no ending. The searcher has read the news, seen the photo, and now has to decide what to do — and if the post does not tell them, they will do nothing. Every post should end with one clear action, matched to the post. An offer should say "Call to book" or "Book online", with the button that does it. An event should say "Visit on the day" or "Book your place", with the details of how. An update about a new service should say "Call to ask about it" or "Learn more", with a link to the page that explains it. A product post should say "Visit the shop" or "Order online", with the link that takes the searcher there. Use the button the profile provides, and check that it works — a button that leads to a wrong number, a dead link or a booking page that does not load will lose the customer the post just won. Keep the action to one per post. A post that says "call, visit, message, book and learn more" is a post that asks for everything and gets nothing. And match the action to the searcher's situation: a person searching on a phone, away from home, is more likely to call or message than to visit a website, so a call or message button is often the right next step for a local post. The test for a post is simple: after reading it, does the searcher know exactly what to do next? If the answer is yes, the post is finished. If the answer is no, add the next step before publishing.

How to Tell Whether Your Posts Are Working

Posts can be measured, and they should be — not with complicated reports, but with the simple question: did more of the right people contact us? In the profile's own insights, look at the actions searchers took: calls, direction requests, website visits and messages. Compare the weeks when you posted with the weeks when you did not, over a few months rather than a few days, because one week is too short to judge. Look at which kinds of post got a response: did offers bring calls, did event posts bring visits, did updates bring website clicks? Then make more of the kind that worked, and fewer of the kind that did not. Also listen to the customers themselves. If a new customer says "I saw your post about the offer", or "I saw the photo of the job you finished", that is evidence the posts are doing their job, and it is worth noting which post they meant. Do not judge posts by views alone. A post can be viewed by many people and bring in no customers, if it says nothing worth acting on. Judge them by the actions they led to — the calls, the bookings, the visits — and by whether the profile, as a whole, is bringing in the kind of customer you want. If the posts are not working after a few months of regular posting, the problem is usually not the frequency. It is the content: the posts are too vague, the photos are not real, or the next step is missing. Fix those three things before posting more often, and check the whole profile against our local SEO audit checklist, because a post cannot fix a profile whose hours, categories or reviews are wrong.

Mistakes That Make Posts Invisible

The mistakes that waste Business Profile posts are usually small, and they are all avoidable. Posting once and never again, so the profile goes quiet for months. Writing posts that say nothing specific — "we are here to help", "quality you can trust" — which give a searcher no reason to choose this business. Using a stock photo, or no photo at all, so the post looks like every other post. Making every post an offer, so the business looks permanently on sale. Hiding the condition of an offer, so the customers it brings feel misled. Forgetting the next step, so the searcher reads the post and does nothing. Posting about something that has already happened, so the profile looks out of date. Writing the post for other businesses, in trade language a customer would not use, instead of for the customer standing in the shop. And copying a competitor's posts, so the profile sounds like every other business in the area. Each of these is easy to check before publishing: is it specific, is it recent, is it real, does it have a photo of my own business, and does it end with one clear next step? If the answer to all five is yes, the post is worth publishing. If the answer to any one is no, fix it first — a post that passes those five tests will do more for a profile than a dozen posts that do not.

Start this week, with one post. Choose the news your customers would most want to know — the offer, the event, the job finished, the service newly available — write it in three or four plain sentences, choose one real photo, and end with one next step. Publish it, check it on your own phone, and then do the same next week. In a month, your profile will look alive. In three months, it will have a record of recent work that a searcher can trust. And in a year, it will be the profile in your area that looks like a business worth choosing — not because of any single post, but because of the habit of posting. That habit is the whole strategy, and it starts with the one post you write today. Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I post on Google Business Profile?

Once a week is a realistic rhythm for most small businesses, and it is enough to keep the profile looking active. A business with frequent news — a restaurant with a changing menu, a shop with weekly offers, a service business with seasonal work — can post more often, but the quality matters more than the count. One useful post a week, every week, will do more for a profile than five posts in one week followed by silence for two months. Set a fixed day, keep a small list of ideas ready, and treat the weekly post as part of the routine, like opening the shop.

What should a Google Business Profile post say?

A good post says one thing clearly, in words a customer would understand, and ends with a next step. Name the offer, the news or the event in the first line, give the detail a customer needs — the dates, the area, what is included — and finish with what to do next: call, book, visit, or send a message. Keep it short enough to read in a few seconds on a phone, and put the most important words first, because a searcher scanning a profile will read the first line and decide whether to read the rest. If the post needs more detail, put it on your website and link to it, rather than writing a long post nobody finishes.

Do Google Business Profile posts improve my ranking?

No promise can be made about ranking, and posts should not be written for ranking alone. What posts do is give a searcher who has already found your profile a reason to choose you: recent news shows the business is active, an offer gives a reason to act now, and a photo shows what the business actually looks like. A profile that is kept up to date looks alive and trustworthy next to a profile that has not changed in a year. That is the honest value of posting — it helps the searchers you already reach decide to contact you, rather than guaranteeing that more searchers will find you.

What kind of photo works best in a Business Profile post?

A real photo of your own business, your own work or your own team, taken in good light and cropped so the subject fills the frame. A photo of a finished job, a product on display, the inside of the shop, or the team at work tells a searcher more in one glance than any stock image. Avoid text-heavy images, because the words in an image are hard to read at the small size a post appears at on a phone. Use one clear photo per post, make sure it matches what the post says, and keep the same look across posts so the profile feels like one business rather than a collection of unrelated images.
