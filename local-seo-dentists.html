Local SEO for Dentists: More Patients from Google

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

Most new dental patients do not start by asking a friend, and they do not start on a practice's website. They start on Google, usually on a phone, usually with a problem — a toothache, a chipped tooth, a child who needs a check-up, a move to a new area — and they type the treatment and the place together: "dentist near me", "emergency dentist", "teeth whitening", followed by the town or neighbourhood. The practices they see first are the ones with a complete Google Business Profile, recent reviews from real patients, a website that answers the questions a nervous patient asks, and pages for the treatments people actually search for. This guide builds that presence, piece by piece, for a dental practice: the profile, the reviews, the website, the treatment pages, the local content, and the routine that keeps it all working. Nothing here promises a ranking or a number of patients. What it promises is a practice that is easy to find, easy to trust, and easy to book — and for a local service where trust decides everything, that is the work that matters. If the profile is not set up yet, start with our Google Business Profile setup guide; for the wider local picture, read our Google Map Pack guide and our local SEO audit checklist alongside this one.

A dental patient searching on Google is not browsing. They are deciding, often quickly, and often while they are worried. The person with a toothache wants a practice that can see them soon, that is close, and that will not make the experience worse. The parent looking for a children's dentist wants a practice that is gentle, patient and used to nervous children. The person who has just moved wants a practice they can register with, that answers the phone, and that explains its prices plainly. Each of those searchers is comparing a small number of practices, and they are comparing them on the evidence Google shows: the profile, the photos, the reviews, and the first lines of the website. A practice that looks complete, recent and human will win that comparison more often than a practice that looks empty, out of date, or like every other practice in the area. Local SEO for dentists is therefore not a technical exercise. It is the work of making the practice's real quality visible to a stranger, in the few seconds that stranger spends deciding. The profile must say the right things. The reviews must show real patients, recently, saying what the practice is actually good at. The website must answer the questions a patient asks before they book: what will happen, will it hurt, how long will it take, what will it cost, and how do I book? And the practice must keep doing all of it, week after week, because a profile that was perfect a year ago and has not changed since tells a searcher the practice may not be paying attention now. This guide works through each piece in the order a practice should build it, starting with the profile that every local search shows first.

How Dental Patients Actually Search — and What They Compare

Dental searches fall into three groups, and a practice needs to be ready for all three, because each group is a different kind of patient. The first group is the urgent search: a toothache, a broken tooth, a lost filling, a swelling. The patient wants help soon, close by, and from a practice that will answer. They search on a phone, they look at the opening hours and the phone number first, and they call the practice that looks open and reachable. The second group is the treatment search: whitening, braces, implants, veneers, a check-up, a hygienist appointment. The patient is not in pain, but they have decided to do something, and they are comparing practices on the treatment, the approach and the price. They search the treatment and the place together, they read the treatment page, and they look at the reviews for patients who had the same treatment. The third group is the new-patient search: someone who has moved, changed jobs, or left a previous practice, and who wants a regular dentist. They search for a dentist in the area, they compare several practices, and they choose on trust — reviews, photos, the feel of the website, and whether the practice seems welcoming to new patients and to nervous patients. All three groups compare the same evidence, in the same order: the profile's completeness, the recency and tone of the reviews, the photos, and the website's first lines. A practice that serves all three groups will have a profile that shows it can handle emergencies, treatment pages that answer the treatment questions, and a new-patient page that says plainly how to register, what the first visit involves, and what it costs. A practice that only serves one group — only emergencies, or only cosmetic work — will miss the patients in the other two, and will look incomplete to a searcher who wanted the other kind of help. Build for all three, and let each kind of patient find the page that was written for them.

Your Google Business Profile: The Front Door of the Practice

The Google Business Profile is the first thing a local searcher sees, and for many patients it is the only thing they will read before calling. It must therefore be complete, accurate and human. Start with the basics, and check them against the practice itself: the exact practice name, as it appears on the door and on the website; the correct street address, with the entrance and parking notes a first-time visitor needs; the phone number that is answered during opening hours; and the opening hours as they really are, including any variation for holidays and any days the practice closes early. A single wrong detail — a phone number that rings unanswered, hours that say open when the practice is closed — will lose a patient who was ready to book, and will teach the searcher not to trust the profile next time. Then add the treatments the practice offers, using the words patients use. A patient searches for "teeth whitening", "braces", "dental implants" and "emergency dentist", not for the clinical names of the procedures, so the profile should carry both: the patient words first, the clinical terms where they help. Add the details that answer a patient's practical questions: whether new patients are being taken on, whether emergency appointments are available, whether the practice treats children, whether step-free access is available, and how to book — by phone, online, or both. Write the practice description in plain words, saying who the practice is for, what it is known for, and what a new patient can expect at the first visit, rather than a list of qualifications a patient cannot judge. The profile is not a one-time job. Check it every month, update the hours before every holiday, and add a new treatment the week it is offered, because a profile that is out of date is worse than a profile that was never finished — it tells the searcher the practice is not paying attention.

Reviews: The Trust Signal No Advert Can Buy

For a dental practice, reviews are not a marketing extra. They are the evidence a nervous patient uses to decide whether to trust a stranger with their teeth, and they are the first thing most searchers read after the profile itself. A practice with a steady flow of recent, specific reviews — from patients who name the treatment, the dentist, and the experience — will be chosen over a practice with more reviews that are older, vaguer, or all from the same month. The way to earn reviews is simple, and it is the way most practices fail to do: ask at the right moment, in person, and make it easy. The right moment is when the patient is happy — after a painless treatment, after a problem has been solved, after a nervous patient has got through a visit they were dreading. A short, honest request from the dentist or the receptionist, at the desk, with a card or a link the patient can use on their phone before they leave, will bring in more reviews than any email sent days later, when the visit is forgotten and the patient is busy. Never offer a reward for a review, never write a review for a patient, and never ask only the happiest patients while avoiding the rest — a review profile that is all praise and no criticism will be read as managed, and a practice that only asks the happy patients will miss the honest feedback that makes the practice better. Reply to every review. Thank the patient, by name where the review gives one, and say something specific about what they mentioned, so the reply reads as a real person answering a real patient rather than a copied thank-you. Answer a critical review calmly, briefly, and without arguing or sharing patient details: acknowledge the experience, apologise that it fell short, and invite the patient to contact the practice directly so the matter can be looked into. A calm, human reply to a critical review does more for trust than a dozen five-star reviews, because it shows the searcher how the practice handles a problem — and every patient knows that problems happen, and wants to know the practice will handle theirs well. Our reviews guide covers the whole routine in detail, including the wording to use when asking and the way to handle the difficult reviews. Build the routine into the working week, so that asking for a review is as normal as booking the next appointment, and the reviews will keep coming without a campaign or a push.

Photos That Calm a Nervous Patient Before They Arrive

A dental patient who has not visited before is imagining the worst: a cold room, a bright light, a chair they cannot get out of, a stranger in a mask. Photos are the practice's chance to replace that imagination with reality, before the patient ever arrives. The photos that do that job are not stock photos of smiling models with perfect teeth. They are real photos of this practice: the outside of the building and the entrance, so the patient can recognise it when they arrive; the reception area, so they know what the first minute will feel like; the treatment rooms, clean, calm and well lit; and the team, in their real working clothes, with the names the patient will hear on the phone. A photo of the dentist who will treat them, with a short introduction on the website, turns a stranger into a person, and a person is much easier to trust than a practice name. Add photos of the details that answer a patient's worries: the waiting area, the children's corner if there is one, the parking or the nearest stop, the entrance for patients who use a wheelchair or a pushchair. Update the photos when the practice changes — a new room, a new team member, a redecoration — because photos from years ago will be noticed, and a practice whose photos do not match its rooms will be doubted on everything else. Avoid the two photo mistakes that damage dental profiles most: using no photos at all, which leaves the patient's imagination to fill the gap with the worst version, and using stock photos, which tell the searcher the practice did not think its own rooms were worth showing. One honest set of photos, taken in good light on a quiet day, will do more for a nervous patient than any amount of advertising, because it answers the question every nervous patient asks first: what will it actually be like when I get there?

Your Website: The Questions Every Patient Asks Before Booking

The website is where the patient goes after the profile has done its work, and it has one job: to answer the questions that stand between the searcher and the booking. A dental website that only lists treatments and qualifications will not do that job, because it answers the questions dentists ask, not the questions patients ask. The patient's questions are practical and emotional at once: what will happen at my first visit, will it hurt, how long will it take, how many visits will I need, what will it cost, how do I pay, and how do I book? Every page of the website should answer the questions that belong to it, in plain words, before it asks the patient to do anything. The homepage should say, in its first lines, who the practice is for, where it is, whether it is taking new patients, and how to book — because a patient who has to hunt for the phone number will call the practice that put it first. The new-patient page should describe the first visit step by step, from arrival to the examination to the plan that follows, and should say what to bring, how long to allow, and what the first visit costs or how the cost is worked out. The nervous-patient page — and every practice should have one — should say plainly what the practice does for patients who are anxious, in pain, or embarrassed about the state of their teeth, because the patient who is embarrassed will not ask, and will only book if the website says, without being asked, that they will be treated kindly and without judgement. The contact page should carry the address, the phone number, the opening hours, a map, and the parking and public transport details, because a patient who cannot find the practice cannot become a patient. Check the website on a phone, because most local searchers are on one, and check that the phone number can be tapped to call, the booking button works, and the pages load quickly. A website that is hard to use on a phone will lose the patient the profile just won, and the practice will never know, because the patient will simply call the next practice on the list.

Treatment Pages: One Page Per Treatment, Written for Patients

A practice needs a page for every treatment it wants to be found for, and each page should be written for the patient who is considering that treatment, not for other dentists and not for a search engine. The structure is the same for every treatment, and it follows the patient's questions in the order they ask them. First, what the treatment is, in one plain paragraph: what it does, who it is for, and what problem it solves. Second, what happens: the steps, in order, in plain language, including how many visits are usually needed and how long each one takes. Third, the question every patient asks and few pages answer honestly: will it hurt, and what is done to make it comfortable? Fourth, the aftercare: what to expect afterwards, what to do and what to avoid, and when to call the practice. Fifth, the cost: the price, or the way the price is worked out if it varies, and the payment options, including any payment plans. Sixth, the next step: how to book a consultation, and what that consultation involves. Write the page in the words the patient uses. A patient searches for "teeth whitening", "braces for adults", "dental implants" and "root canal", and the page should use those words in its title and its first paragraph, with the clinical terms explained where they appear. Add a short section answering the questions patients actually ask about that treatment — the ones the receptionist hears every week — because those questions are the search phrases the page will be found for, and because a page that answers them will be trusted more than a page that only describes the procedure. Start with the treatments the practice most wants to grow, write those pages first, and add the rest over time. A practice with five excellent treatment pages will be found and trusted more than a practice with twenty thin pages that say the same sentence about every treatment. And keep the pages honest: if a treatment is not suitable for everyone, say who it suits and who should ask about an alternative, because a patient who is told honestly that a treatment is not for them will trust the practice's recommendation when it is.

Local Pages and Content: The Area, the Community, the Real Practice

A dental practice serves a place, and the website should show that it belongs to that place. A local page — or a small set of local pages — that names the area, the neighbourhoods served, and the landmarks patients use to find the practice will help the searcher who types the treatment and the place together, and will reassure the searcher who wants a practice that knows their area. Write the local pages from real knowledge: the areas the practice serves, the schools and employers nearby, the community events the practice supports, and the practical details a local patient needs, such as the bus routes, the parking, and the walk from the station. Avoid the generic local page that names the town ten times and says nothing about it, because a searcher will recognise it as filler and a search engine will treat it the same way. The local content that works best for a dental practice is the content that shows the practice is part of the community: the school visit, the sports club sponsorship, the charity event, the open day, the talk at the community centre. Write about those things when they happen, with real photos, and the website will accumulate a record of a practice that is present in its area, which is exactly what a new patient wants to see. Answer the local questions patients ask too: whether the practice treats patients from neighbouring towns, how far patients travel, and what to do if you are visiting the area and need emergency treatment. A practice that answers those questions plainly will be found by the visitors and the new arrivals who need a dentist most urgently, and who have no local knowledge to guide them. Keep the local pages accurate and current: an event page from two years ago, or a sponsorship that ended, tells the searcher the practice has stopped paying attention to its own community, and that impression will colour everything else they read.

Posts, Questions and Updates: Keeping the Profile Alive

A profile that never changes looks like a practice that has stopped caring, and a profile that changes regularly looks like a practice that is open, busy and paying attention. The weekly post is the simplest way to keep the profile alive, and for a dental practice it is easy to fill, because a practice always has news a patient would want to know: a new treatment, a new team member, a change to opening hours, a seasonal reminder, a community event, a tip for the time of year. Write one short post a week, with one real photo and one next step, following the method in our Google Business Profile posts guide. The posts that work best for dentists are the practical ones: the reminder to book a check-up before a holiday, the explanation of a treatment in plain words, the introduction of a new hygienist, the note about the practice's approach to nervous patients. Answer the questions patients ask on the profile, too, because those questions are visible to every future searcher and the answers are part of the profile's evidence. If a patient asks whether the practice takes emergency appointments, answer plainly and say how to book one. If a patient asks about prices, answer with the way prices are worked out and invite them to call for an exact quote, rather than leaving the question unanswered. Check the profile's questions and messages as part of the weekly routine, and answer them within a working day, because a question left unanswered for weeks tells the searcher the practice does not answer its patients. The routine is small — one post, one check of the questions, one reply to the new reviews — and it is the difference between a profile that works for the practice every day and a profile that was set up once and forgotten.

Bookings: Making the Next Step Effortless

Every piece of local SEO work ends at the same place: the patient trying to book. If booking is hard, the work is wasted, because the patient who has decided to book and then cannot will book somewhere else, and will not come back to try again. Make the next step obvious on every page and on the profile: the phone number, tappable on a phone, in the same place on every page; the online booking link, if the practice offers it, labelled plainly and tested every week; and the message option, if the practice answers messages, with the hours within which a reply can be expected. Say what happens when the patient books: whether they will speak to a person, whether they will get a confirmation, and what they should bring or do before the first visit. For emergency patients, say plainly how to get help outside opening hours, and what to do while waiting, because the patient in pain is the patient who most needs a clear instruction and who will remember the practice that gave it. Train the team to answer the phone the way the website promises: promptly, kindly, and with the information the patient needs, because a website that promises a welcoming practice and a phone that rings unanswered will lose the patient in the gap between the two. Check the booking path yourself, as a patient would: search for the practice, read the profile, visit the website, and try to book, on a phone, in the evening, when the practice is closed. If any step is confusing, slow or broken, fix it before writing another page of content, because the booking path is the practice's real front door, and everything else in this guide exists to bring patients to it.

Measuring What Matters: Enquiries, Bookings and the Right Patients

Dental local SEO should be measured by the patients it brings, not by the positions it reaches. The numbers that matter are the ones the practice already keeps: the calls that ask for an appointment, the online bookings, the new patients who register, and the treatments that are booked. Compare those numbers over a few months, not over a few days, and compare the months when the profile, the reviews and the pages were kept up to date with the months when they were not. Ask new patients how they found the practice, and note the answer, because a patient who says "I found you on Google and your reviews convinced me" is evidence the work is doing its job, and a patient who says "I drove past" is evidence of something else entirely. Look at the profile's own insights — the calls, the direction requests, the website visits — and look at which kinds of post and which pages brought the enquiries, so the practice can do more of what worked and less of what did not. Do not judge the work by a single search position on a single day, because positions move, different searchers see different results, and a position without enquiries is a vanity number. Judge it by whether the practice is hearing from the kind of patients it wants: the new patients, the treatment patients, the families, the nervous patients who needed reassurance and found it. If the enquiries are not coming after a few months of steady work, the problem is usually not the frequency of posting. It is one of the basics: the profile is incomplete, the reviews have stalled, the treatment pages do not answer the patient's questions, or the booking step is broken. Mistakes That Cost Dental Practices Patients

The mistakes that waste dental local SEO are usually small, and they are all avoidable. A profile with the wrong hours, so the patient who arrives finds the practice closed. A phone number that rings unanswered, so the patient calls the next practice. Reviews that stopped two years ago, so the searcher wonders whether the practice is still open. Stock photos, so the searcher cannot picture the real practice. A website that lists treatments in clinical language, so the patient cannot tell what any of them involves. No treatment pages, so the patient searching for a treatment finds a competitor instead. No mention of nervous patients, so the anxious patient books elsewhere. A booking button that does not work on a phone, so the patient who decided to book cannot. Posts that stopped months ago, so the profile looks abandoned. And the worst mistake of all: promising a result — a position, a number of patients, a timescale — that no honest practice can guarantee, and then losing the patient's trust when the promise is not kept. Check for these before anything else: is the profile complete and accurate, are the reviews recent and answered, do the photos show the real practice, do the treatment pages answer the patient's questions, and does the booking step work on a phone? If the answer to all five is yes, the practice is doing the work that matters. If the answer to any one is no, fix it first — a practice that passes those five tests will be found, trusted and booked more often than a practice that spends its time on anything else. Start this week, with the profile. Check the hours, the phone number, the treatments and the photos, ask the next happy patient for a review, and write the first treatment page for the treatment the practice most wants to grow. Then do the same next week, and the week after. In a few months, the practice will have a profile that looks alive, a set of reviews that reads like real patients, and pages that answer the questions patients actually ask — and the patients who find the practice on Google will arrive already trusting it, which is the best possible start to any appointment. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does local SEO take for a dental practice?

There is no fixed timescale, and no honest answer can promise one. The basics — a complete and accurate profile, correct details everywhere, a website that answers the questions patients ask — can be put in place in a few weeks, and the effect of those basics is often visible quickly, because many practices have never done them properly. The deeper work — a steady flow of reviews, helpful local pages, and a record of answering patients well — builds over months, and it is that record that keeps a practice visible over time. Judge the work by the enquiries and bookings it brings, over a few months, rather than by a single week's position.

What should a dental practice put on its Google Business Profile?

The exact practice name, the correct address and phone number, the opening hours as they really are, the treatments offered, and real photos of the practice, the team and the treatment rooms. Add the services patients search for — check-ups, hygiene, fillings, whitening, orthodontics, emergency appointments, and any specialist treatments — using the words patients use, not only the clinical terms. Keep the hours and the services up to date, answer the questions patients ask on the profile, and post a short update at least once a week, so the profile looks like a practice that is open, active and paying attention.

How can a dentist get more Google reviews without asking awkwardly?

Ask at the right moment, in person, and make it easy. The right moment is when a patient is happy — after a painless treatment, after a problem has been solved, or at the end of a visit that went well. A short, honest request from the dentist or the receptionist, with a card or a link the patient can use on their phone, works far better than a long email sent days later. Never offer a reward for a review, and never write reviews for patients. Reply to every review, thank the patient by name where the review gives one, and answer a critical review calmly and briefly, inviting the patient to contact the practice directly.

Does a dental practice need a page for every treatment?

A practice needs a page for every treatment it wants to be found for, written for the patient, not for other dentists. A patient searching for a treatment wants to know what it involves, whether it will hurt, how long it takes, how many visits it needs, and what it costs or how the cost is worked out. One page that answers those questions for one treatment will serve the practice far better than one long services page that mentions every treatment in a sentence. Start with the treatments the practice most wants to grow, write those pages first, and add the rest over time.
