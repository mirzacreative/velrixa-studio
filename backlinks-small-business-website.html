How to Get Backlinks for a Small Business Website

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

A backlink is a link from another website to yours, and search engines treat it as a recommendation: a sign that another site found your website worth referring to. For a small business, the links that matter most are the honest ones — local listings and directories, supplier and partner pages, local news and community mentions, and links to genuinely useful content on your website. A few links from real, relevant, trusted websites are worth far more than many links from sites nobody visits, and buying links is not a shortcut: it is against Google's guidelines, the links usually come from low-quality sites, and a website caught buying them can be penalised. The work in this guide is slow and unglamorous — checking listings, asking partners, writing content worth linking to — and it builds over months. Nothing here promises a ranking; the businesses that benefit from backlinks are the ones that earn a few good ones every month and keep at it.

When another website links to yours, it is doing more than sending a visitor your way. It is telling search engines that your website is worth referring to — a recommendation, in the language search engines understand. That is why backlinks are one of the signals search engines use when deciding which websites to show first: a website that other sites link to looks more trusted than one that no site links to. For a small business, that creates a frustrating gap. A big company gets links without asking, because journalists, bloggers and customers link to it as a matter of course. A small business has to earn every link, and the advice it usually finds is either too vague — "create great content" — or too dangerous — "buy links from this list". This guide is the middle path: the legitimate, practical ways a small business earns backlinks, one at a time, from the relationships and the content it already has. Local listings, supplier and partner pages, community content, local press and digital PR — each one is a real method, each one is free or nearly free, and none of them risks the website they are meant to help. What a Backlink Is — and What Makes One Worth Having

A backlink is simply a link from another website to yours. When a directory lists your business with a link to your website, that is a backlink. When a supplier lists you on its "where to buy" page, that is a backlink. When a local newspaper mentions your business and links to your site, that is a backlink. Search engines treat these links as recommendations, and three things decide how much a link is worth: whether the linking site is real and trusted, whether it is relevant to your business, and whether the link looks natural. A link from a real local newspaper to a local business is worth a lot, because the newspaper is trusted, it is local, and the link makes sense. A link from a trade association to a member business is worth a lot for the same reasons. A link from a website that exists only to sell links, on a topic that has nothing to do with your business, is worth very little — and can do harm, because search engines can tell the difference. The practical consequence is simple: a small business does not need many links. It needs the right ones. A dozen links from real local sites, suppliers, partners and publications will do more for a small business than a hundred links from sites nobody visits. And every method in this guide is judged by that test: is the linking site real, is it relevant, and would the link exist even if search engines did not exist? If the answer to all three is yes, it is a link worth having.

Local Listings and Directories: The Foundation

The first backlinks a small business should earn are the easiest ones: its listings. A listing is a mention of your business — name, address and phone number — on another website, and most good listings include a link to your website. Start with the big, trusted directories and review sites for your country, and make sure each listing is accurate and complete, with the same name, address and phone number as your Google Business Profile. Then the local ones: your town's business directory, your chamber of commerce, your trade association, the local business listings your council or tourism board keeps. These are exactly the links that are most relevant to a local business, and they are also the ones that confirm to search engines that the business is real and that its details are what its profile says they are. If your Business Profile is not set up yet, start with our Google Business Profile setup guide — the listings and the profile reinforce each other. A few rules for this step: only list the business where it genuinely belongs, keep the details identical everywhere, and do not pay for hundreds of low-quality directory listings. A handful of accurate listings on trusted sites is worth far more than a hundred on sites nobody visits, and a directory that charges for a link is not the same as a directory that lists your business because it is a real business in the area. Check the listings once a quarter, because directories change, old phone numbers resurface, and a listing created years ago by someone else can still be live with wrong details.

Supplier, Partner and Client Pages

The businesses you already work with are the most overlooked source of backlinks — and the easiest to earn, because the relationship already exists. If you sell a supplier's products, ask to be listed on their "stockists" or "where to buy" page. If you are a supplier, list the businesses that stock your products, and ask them to return the favour. If you work with a partner — an accountant who refers clients to you, a designer who builds websites for your customers, a tradesperson you recommend — ask whether they would link to your website from theirs, and offer the same in return where it makes sense. A link between two businesses that actually work together is the most natural link there is, and search engines read it that way. The same goes for clients, with care: if you have done good work for a client who has a website, ask whether they would mention you on their site — a case study, a testimonial with a link, a "built by" credit on a website you designed. Not every client will say yes, and you should never make a link a condition of the work, but many will, because a link to a supplier or a contractor is a normal part of doing business. Keep a simple list of everyone you work with — suppliers, partners, clients, trade contacts — and work through it once, asking politely and once only. One ask per relationship, and no chasing: a link that has to be begged for is not a natural link, and the relationship is worth more than the link.

Community Content That Earns Links

Some of the best backlinks a small business can earn come from the community it is part of — and they come from doing things that are worth mentioning, not from asking for links. Sponsor a local team, a school event or a charity fundraiser, and the organisation will usually list its sponsors on its website, with a link. Join your trade association and your local business group, and their member directories will list you. Speak at a local event, run a workshop, or host a community gathering, and the event page will link to your website. Write a guide that answers a question your customers actually ask — the kind of guide this blog publishes — and other sites will link to it when their own readers ask the same question. The pattern is the same in every case: do something useful or visible in the real world, and the links follow, because real organisations link to real businesses that do real things. That is also why these links are the most valuable kind: they come from real sites, they are relevant to your area and your trade, and they would exist even if search engines did not. A single sponsorship link from a local organisation's website can be worth more than a dozen links from directories, because it is a link no competitor can copy without doing the same work. Start with the organisations you are already part of, and the events you already attend — the links are usually one email away.

Digital PR: Local News, Data and Stories

Local news sites and blogs need stories, and a small business that gives them one earns a link that money cannot buy. The stories that work are the ones with a local angle and a real fact in them: a business opening, a milestone, a new service, a survey of your customers, a piece of data only you have. If you run a cafe, a survey of your customers' favourite orders is a story. If you run a trade business, the most common problems you are called out for this year is a story. If you have hired three people this year, that is a story. The method is simple: write a short, honest press note — a paragraph about what happened, a quote from you, and a link to your website — and send it to the local news sites and blogs that cover your area, with a photo if you have one. Do not send it to national outlets, and do not send the same note to fifty sites at once: pick the handful that actually cover your town and your trade, and write each email to the person who covers that beat. Most will not reply, and that is normal. The ones that do will usually link to your website when they publish the story, because that is how news sites work — and a link from a local news site is one of the strongest links a small business can earn. A second, quieter form of digital PR is to be a source: when a journalist or a blogger asks a question about your trade, answer it well and quickly, and they will often link to you as the source. Watch for those requests in your trade groups and local business networks, and answer the ones you can answer honestly.

Helpful Content Worth Linking To

The links a website earns on its own, without anyone asking, almost always point at its most useful pages — and that is a reason to publish content that is genuinely useful to people who are not your customers yet. A guide that answers a question your customers actually ask, a checklist that saves someone an hour, a calculator that works out a cost, a comparison that is honest about the trade-offs: these are the pages other sites link to when their own readers ask the same question. Our own guides work this way — this blog exists because the questions it answers are the questions small businesses actually ask, and the links it earns come from other sites pointing their readers at the answer. The content that earns links has a few things in common: it answers one question properly, it is specific enough to be useful, it is kept up to date, and it is on a page that is easy to link to — one topic, one clear title, one URL. A page about one service in one area is more linkable than a page that lists everything you do, because a site linking to it knows exactly what its readers will find. And the content does not have to be long to be useful: a short, accurate checklist can earn more links than a long article that says nothing new. As you publish, think about who would link to each page — a supplier, a local site, a blogger, a customer — and write the page for them as well as for your customers. If you are unsure what to write, our guide to getting cited by AI search explains the same principle from the other side: content that answers a question clearly, with facts a machine or a person can check, is the content that gets referenced.

Guest Contributions, Done Honestly

Writing for another website can earn a good backlink — if it is done honestly. The honest version is simple: you write a useful article for a website your customers actually read — a trade publication, a local business site, a supplier's blog — and the article links back to your website where it is relevant, usually in the author note. The article has to be worth publishing on its own: a real guide, a real case study, a real opinion based on your experience — not a thin piece written only to carry a link. The dishonest version is the one to avoid: paying for a post on a site that exists to sell posts, writing the same article for many sites, or stuffing links into a piece that does not need them. Those links are easy for search engines to recognise, and they do not help. Before you write for a site, check that it is real: does it publish regularly, does it have real readers, would you be proud to have your name on it? If the answer is yes, pitch one specific article, not a vague offer to "contribute", and write it as well as you would write for your own website. One good guest article a quarter is worth more than a dozen thin ones, and the relationship with the site's editor is worth more than the link — because an editor who trusts you will invite you back.

What to Avoid: Buying Links and Other Shortcuts

The shortcuts are tempting because they are fast, and they are dangerous for the same reason. Buying links is against Google's guidelines, and the links it buys are usually from low-quality sites that exist only to sell links — sites with no real readers, no real content, and hundreds of outgoing links to unrelated businesses. Search engines can detect those patterns, and they can penalise the website the links point to: a bought link can do the opposite of what you paid for. The same goes for link exchanges at scale, for automated directory submissions that list your business on hundreds of sites nobody visits, and for private blog networks — groups of sites built only to link to each other. All of them share the same flaw: the links would not exist if search engines did not exist, and search engines can tell. The honest test is the one from the start of this guide: would this link exist anyway, from a real site, for a real reason? If the answer is no, do not buy it, do not trade for it, and do not build it. A small business that earns a few real links a month for a year will end up with a stronger link profile than a business that buys a hundred links in a week — and it will not be living with the risk of a penalty that wipes out the gain. If an email offers you links for a price, delete it. If a service promises a set number of links in a set time, walk away. The work is slower, but it is the only version that lasts.

A 90-Day Backlink Routine for a Small Business

If you want a plan instead of a list, here is one that takes a few hours a month. Month one: fix the foundation. Check every local listing and directory, correct the details, and claim the ones that belong to you. Check your Google Business Profile against our setup guide and your reviews against our reviews guide, because the profile and the listings reinforce each other. Then make the supplier and partner list, and send one polite email to each business you work with, asking for a link where it makes sense. Month two: publish one genuinely useful piece of content — a guide, a checklist, a calculator — and tell the people who might link to it: your suppliers, your trade association, your local business group. Then write one short press note about something real that happened in your business, and send it to the local news sites that cover your area. Month three: review what worked. Which listings link to you now, which partners said yes, which piece of content earned a link? Then repeat the cycle: one new piece of content, one new press note, one new ask to a partner, and a check of the listings. Keep a simple record — the site, the link, the date — so you can see the total growing, and so you know which methods work for your business. And check the whole picture once a quarter against our local SEO audit checklist, so the backlinks stay matched to a website that is worth linking to. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a backlink?

A backlink is a link from another website to yours. When a directory, a supplier, a local news site or another business links to your website, that link is a backlink. Search engines treat backlinks as a signal that other sites find your website worth referring to — a kind of recommendation. A few links from real, relevant, trusted websites are worth far more than many links from sites nobody visits.

How many backlinks does a small business need?

There is no number to aim for, because the value of a backlink depends on where it comes from, not how many there are. A small business in a quiet market can do well with a modest set of good links — its local listings, its suppliers and partners, a few local mentions and a handful of links to its best content. A business in a competitive market will need more, and it will need them over a longer time. The honest way to think about it is not a target number but a habit: earn a few good links every month, and the total takes care of itself.

Is buying backlinks a good idea?

No. Buying links is against Google's guidelines, and the links it buys are usually from low-quality sites that exist only to sell links. Google can detect those patterns and can penalise the website they point to — which means a bought link can do the opposite of what you paid for. A bought link is also not a real recommendation: it does not bring visitors, it does not build trust, and it disappears when you stop paying. Earn links from real relationships and real content instead.

How long does it take for backlinks to help?

Backlinks help gradually, not overnight. A new link needs to be found by search engines first, and the effect builds as more good links accumulate over months. A local listing link can start helping quickly because the listing itself is already trusted, while a link from a new piece of content may take weeks to be noticed. The businesses that benefit most from backlinks are the ones that earn them steadily for months, not the ones that chase a quick batch and stop.
