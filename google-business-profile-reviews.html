Google Business Profile Reviews: Get More 5 Stars

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

Reviews are the part of your Google Business Profile that customers trust most — and the part most small businesses leave to chance. Getting more 5-star reviews is not a trick: ask happy customers at the right time, make it easy with your review link, and reply to every review you receive, good and bad. Never buy reviews, never offer payment for them, and never post fake ones — Google removes them, and a business caught doing it loses trust it cannot buy back. Reviews also feed local rankings: a profile with a steady flow of recent, genuine reviews and thoughtful replies looks active and reliable to Google and to the customers choosing between you and the business next door.

When a customer searches for a plumber, a dentist, a cafe or a designer near them, the first thing they look at after the business name is the star rating — and then they read the reviews. Two businesses can offer the same service at the same price, and the one with forty recent, genuine reviews will win the call over the one with three reviews from two years ago. Reviews do two jobs at once: they convince the customer, and they tell Google the business is active, trusted and worth showing. The good news is that getting more reviews is mostly a habit, not a budget. This guide covers when and how to ask, how to use your review link, how to reply to every review — good and bad — what never to do, and how reviews feed your local rankings. Why Reviews Matter More Than You Think

Reviews matter because customers trust other customers more than they trust a business talking about itself. A star rating is a summary, but the written reviews are the detail: what the service was like, whether the business turned up on time, whether the price was fair, whether a problem was fixed. A customer choosing between two businesses will read the recent reviews of both and pick the one whose customers sound happier. Reviews also matter to Google. A profile with a steady flow of recent reviews looks alive; a profile whose newest review is two years old looks abandoned, even if the business is busy. The words in reviews matter too — when customers mention the service, the area, or the problem you solved, those words add to what Google understands about the business. That does not mean you should coach customers on what to write; it means that genuine reviews from real customers naturally contain the words that describe your business. And reviews are a feedback loop: read them for patterns, and you will learn what your business does well and what it needs to fix, straight from the people who paid for it.

When to Ask — and Who to Ask

Timing is most of the job. The best moment to ask for a review is a day or two after the service is finished, when the customer is happy and the experience is fresh. Ask too early — before the job is done — and the customer has nothing to review. Ask too late — a month later — and the moment has passed; the customer has moved on and the request feels random. For a shop or cafe, the right moment is at the till or in a follow-up message the same day. For a tradesperson, it is the day after the job is finished, once the customer has had a chance to use the repair or see the finished work. For a service business, it is after the first result the customer can see. Ask the customers who are happy — not by filtering out the unhappy ones, but by asking everyone who had a good experience, as a matter of routine. If you only ask your favourite customers, you will get a skewed picture; if you ask everyone, the reviews will reflect the real business. And ask once. A single polite request is welcome; repeated reminders are not, and they turn a happy customer into an annoyed one.

How to Ask: The Words and the Review Link

Google gives every Business Profile a short review link that takes a customer straight to the review form for your business — no searching, no navigating. Find it in your Business Profile dashboard under "Get more reviews" or by searching for your business on Google and clicking the review option. Use that link everywhere it makes sense: in a follow-up text message, in the receipt or thank-you email, on a small card at the till, or as a QR code on an invoice. The shorter the path from your request to the review form, the more reviews you will get — every extra step loses people. Keep the ask itself short and human. A text message that works: "Thanks for choosing us today — if you have a moment, we would really appreciate a quick review of your experience. Here is the link: [link]." An email version: a short thank-you, one sentence asking for an honest review, and the link as a clear button. A card at the till: "Enjoyed your visit? A quick Google review would mean a lot — scan here." Do not ask for five stars, and do not tell the customer what to write. Ask for an honest review of their experience, and let the service earn the stars. And make the request personal: a message that mentions the actual job or visit — "hope the new boiler is working well" — gets far more responses than a generic "please review us".

Replying to Good Reviews

Reply to every positive review, not just the negative ones. A reply to a good review takes thirty seconds and does three things: it thanks the customer, it shows future customers that the business is run by people who care, and it keeps the profile looking active. Keep replies short and specific. Mention the service or the detail the customer praised: "Thanks, Sarah — glad the new kitchen tap is working well, and thanks for the kind words about our team." That is better than a generic "Thanks for your review!" because it shows you actually read it. Use the customer's name when it is there, and sign off with your name or the business name. Do not copy and paste the same reply to every review — customers notice, and it looks automated. Do not use the reply to sell: a thank-you is not an advert. And do not wait weeks to reply; a reply within a few days shows the business is paying attention. If a customer leaves a detailed, enthusiastic review, a warm reply is also a quiet invitation for them to come back — and a returning customer is worth more than a new one.

Replying to Bad Reviews

A bad review feels personal, but it is a public moment — and how you handle it matters more than the review itself. Future customers will read the bad review and then read your reply, and a calm, helpful reply can turn a warning into a recommendation. First, do not reply angry, and do not reply immediately if you are upset: wait, check what actually happened, and then reply once, calmly. Acknowledge the problem specifically — "We're sorry the appointment ran late on Tuesday" — rather than with a generic apology. Say what you have done about it, if you have: "We have changed our scheduling so this should not happen again." Offer to make it right, offline: "Please call us on [number] so we can sort this out." Keep it short, polite, and factual. Never argue with a reviewer in public, never reveal a customer's private details, and never blame the customer. If the review is unfair or factually wrong, correct the fact once, politely, and leave it there — a long public argument makes the business look worse, not the reviewer. And learn from it: if three customers mention the same problem, that is a business problem to fix at the source, not a marketing problem. A single bad review among many good ones does not hurt a business; an unanswered bad review does, because it sits there looking like nobody cares.

What Never to Do: Fake, Bought and Incentivised Reviews

There is a whole industry selling fake Google reviews, and every part of it is a bad idea. Google removes fake reviews, and a business caught buying them can lose its profile or its visibility — and the trust of any customer who notices. The same applies to offering payment, discounts or freebies in exchange for reviews: Google's rules do not allow it, and a customer who was paid to leave a review is not a customer whose opinion anyone should trust. Do not ask only for five-star reviews, do not ask friends and family to post reviews pretending to be customers, and do not review your own business or ask your staff to do so. Do not set up a "review station" in your shop where customers are watched while they write, and do not filter customers by sending happy ones to Google and unhappy ones to a private form — Google calls that review gating, and it is against the rules. The honest route is slower, but it is the only route that lasts: do good work, ask happy customers politely, reply to every review, and let the stars reflect the real business. A profile with thirty genuine reviews is worth far more than a profile with two hundred fake ones — because the genuine ones survive, and the fake ones do not.

How Reviews Feed Local Rankings

Reviews are one of the signals Google uses when it decides which businesses to show in local results — the map pack and the local listings that appear when someone searches for a service near them. Google does not publish exactly how much reviews matter, and no one can honestly promise a specific ranking from reviews alone. What is clear is that the pattern matters: a profile with a steady flow of recent reviews, a healthy average rating, replies from the owner, and reviews that mention the service and the area looks active and reliable. A profile with no recent reviews, no replies, and an old average looks neglected by comparison. Reviews also affect the click: when two businesses appear side by side in the map pack, the one with more reviews and a higher rating gets the tap. That means reviews work twice — once in helping the profile show up, and once in winning the customer who sees it. The practical implication is simple: do not chase a single big push for reviews and then stop. A steady trickle — a few reviews every month, replied to promptly — is better than fifty reviews in one week followed by silence for a year. Consistency is what both Google and customers read as a sign of a business that is still going strong.

A Simple Weekly Review Routine

Reviews need a routine, not a campaign. Once a week, spend ten minutes on this: open your Business Profile and check for new reviews; reply to each one — a short, specific thank-you for the good ones, and a calm, helpful reply for the bad ones. Check the questions and answers section too, and answer anything new. Then check the ask: did you send the review link to every happy customer this week? If a job finished on Tuesday and the link has not gone out yet, send it now, while the experience is still fresh. Once a month, read all the reviews from the last month together and look for patterns: what are customers praising, and what are they complaining about? The praise tells you what to keep doing; the complaints tell you what to fix. Once a quarter, review the whole profile against our local SEO audit checklist — categories, services, photos, hours and reviews together — so nothing drifts out of date. That small weekly habit is the difference between a profile that quietly gathers reviews and one that quietly loses them. Frequently Asked Questions

How do I get more Google reviews for my business?

Ask happy customers directly, at the right time — a day or two after the job is done, when the experience is fresh. Send them your Google review link by text, email or a card, and make it easy: one tap should take them to the review form. Most customers are willing to leave a review when asked politely; the reason most businesses have few reviews is simply that nobody asked.

Is it against Google's rules to ask customers for reviews?

No. Asking customers for an honest review is allowed and expected. What Google does not allow is offering payment, discounts or freebies in exchange for reviews, asking only for five-star reviews, or posting fake reviews. Ask for an honest review, let the service earn the stars, and reply to every review you receive.

Should I reply to every Google review?

Yes. Reply to positive reviews with a short, specific thank-you, and reply to negative reviews calmly and helpfully, addressing the issue the reviewer raised. Replies show future customers that the business listens and cares, and they give you a public chance to fix a problem. A business that replies to reviews looks active and trustworthy; one that never replies looks absent.

Can I remove a bad Google review?

You cannot remove a review yourself just because it is negative. You can flag a review to Google if it breaks Google's rules — for example, if it is fake, spam, or from someone who was never a customer — and Google may remove it after review. For a genuine bad review from a real customer, the right response is a calm public reply and fixing the problem behind it.
