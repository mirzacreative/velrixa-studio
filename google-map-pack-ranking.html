How to Rank in the Google Map Pack

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

The Google Map Pack is the block of three local businesses, shown with a map, that appears at the top of search results when someone looks for a service nearby. Google chooses those three businesses using three factors: proximity (how close the business is to the searcher), relevance (how well the business matches what was searched for), and prominence (how well-known and trusted the business looks). You cannot change where your business is, but you can improve relevance and prominence — and that is where the work is. A complete Google Business Profile, in the right categories with the right services, a steady flow of genuine reviews with replies, consistent name, address and phone number across the web, and local content on your website are the levers that move a business into the Map Pack. Nothing here promises a ranking: in a competitive area it takes months of steady work, and the businesses that win are the ones that keep at it after the quick fixes are done.

When someone searches for a plumber, a dentist, a cafe or a designer near them, the first thing they see is not a list of websites. It is a map, with three businesses picked out on it, each with a name, a star rating, a review count and a distance. That block is the Google Map Pack, and it usually sits above the normal search results — which means it gets the first look, the first tap, and a large share of the calls. For a local business, being in those three spots is worth more than ranking first in the organic results below them. The frustrating part is that there is no single button to press to get in. Google chooses the three businesses using three factors — proximity, relevance and prominence — and the work is different for each one. This guide explains all three, then works through the practical steps that improve each of them: getting the Business Profile complete, earning and replying to reviews, keeping citations consistent, and building local content on your website. What the Map Pack Is — and Why It Matters

The Map Pack is the set of three local business listings Google shows, with a map, at the top of the results page for a local search. A search for "electrician near me" or "best coffee in Manchester" triggers it; a search for a national brand or a general question usually does not. The three businesses get a name, a star rating, the number of reviews, a category, an address or service area, and buttons to call, get directions, or visit the website. On a phone, the Map Pack often fills the whole first screen — the organic results below it are not visible until the searcher scrolls. That placement is why it matters so much: the businesses in the Pack get the calls from people who never scroll past it. It also matters because the Map Pack is a different competition from organic search. A business can rank first in the organic results and still not appear in the Pack, and a business with a weak website can appear in the Pack if its profile is strong. Google decides the Pack from Business Profiles, not from websites alone — which is good news for a small business, because a complete profile and a good reputation cost far less than outranking a big competitor's website. And the Pack changes with the searcher: two people searching for the same service from different streets will see different businesses, because proximity is part of the decision. That means ranking in the Map Pack is not one ranking — it is a set of rankings, one for each place your customers search from.

The Three Ranking Factors: Proximity, Relevance, Prominence

Google says it ranks local results on three factors, and understanding them is the whole strategy, because every task in this guide improves one of the three. Proximity is how close the business is to the person searching — or to the place named in the search. A plumber two streets away will usually beat a plumber across town for a search made from that street. Relevance is how well the business matches what the searcher asked for: a business whose profile lists "emergency plumbing" as a service, in the right category, with the right words in its description, is more relevant to a search for "emergency plumber" than a business that only mentions plumbing in general. Prominence is how well-known and trusted the business looks — its reviews, how many there are and how recent, its mentions across the web, its website, and the links pointing to it. Of the three, proximity is the one you mostly cannot change, relevance is the one you can improve most directly, and prominence is the one that takes the longest to build. A business that is close, clearly relevant, and visibly trusted is hard to beat. A business that is far away can still win if it is far more relevant and far more prominent than the closer options — but it has to work harder. Every section below is filed under one of these three factors, so you can see what each piece of work is actually doing.

Proximity: What You Can and Cannot Change

Proximity is the least flexible of the three factors, and the one most businesses misunderstand. You cannot move your business closer to every searcher, and you should not try to game it — creating fake addresses or listing addresses where you do not operate will get the profile suspended, and a suspension costs far more than any ranking it might have bought. What you can do is make sure Google knows exactly where you are and where you serve. For a business with a shop or office that customers visit, that means a real, accurate address on the profile, verified with Google, with the pin on the map in the right place. Check the pin: if it points at the wrong building or the wrong end of the street, customers will go to the wrong place, and Google will show you to the wrong searchers. For a service-area business — a tradesperson, a cleaner, a designer who visits clients — the profile should list a service area instead of a public address: the towns or districts you actually serve, set in the profile settings. Google hides the home address and shows the service area, and the profile is then shown to searchers inside that area. Do not list areas you do not serve: a searcher outside your real area who calls you and finds out you will not come is a wasted call and a bad review waiting to happen, and a service area that is too wide dilutes the relevance of the profile. The other honest lever on proximity is where you do your work. A business that takes jobs across a whole city will naturally appear in more searches than one that works in a single neighbourhood — so if you genuinely serve a wider area, say so in the profile, and back it up with content on your website about the areas you serve. That is the limit of what proximity allows: be accurate about where you are, be honest about where you serve, and put the rest of your effort into relevance and prominence, where the real gains are.

Relevance: Categories, Services and Keywords

Relevance is the factor you can improve fastest, because it is mostly about the words on your profile. Start with the category. Google lets you choose one primary category and several additional ones, and the primary category is the most important choice on the whole profile: it tells Google what your business is. Choose the category that matches your main service, not the broadest one — "plumber" is better than "contractor" if plumbing is what you do, because a searcher looking for a plumber will be matched to plumbers first. Add additional categories only for services you genuinely offer; a category you do not serve will bring the wrong searchers and dilute the profile. Next, fill in the services list: every service you offer, described in the words customers use, with a short description and, where it makes sense, a price. The services list is searchable and matchable — a business that lists "boiler repair" as a service is more relevant to that search than one that only lists "plumbing" as a category. Then the business description: a plain, honest paragraph that says what you do, who you serve, and where — using the product and service words your customers actually type, not industry jargon. Add the attributes that apply: wheelchair access, free Wi-Fi, appointment needed, women-owned, and the rest — these help Google match the business to searches that mention them, and they help customers choose. Finally, keep the profile's products and posts current. Products with names, prices and photos add more matchable words; regular posts — an offer, a new service, a seasonal note — keep the profile looking active and add fresh words for Google to match. None of this is keyword stuffing: it is describing the business accurately and completely, in the words customers use, so that when someone searches for what you do, Google can see that you do it.

Prominence: Reviews, Citations and Reputation

Prominence is Google's judgement of how well-known and trusted a business is, and it is built from everything the business has done outside its own profile: the reviews it has earned, the mentions of it across the web, the links to its website, and how long it has been around. It is the slowest factor to build, because it depends on real activity over time, and it is also the factor where shortcuts are most dangerous. There is no honest way to buy prominence. Fake reviews get removed, and a business caught buying them can lose its profile. Paid links and fake listings do the same damage in the other direction. The honest way to build prominence is to accumulate the evidence of a real, busy business: a steady flow of genuine reviews, consistent mentions of the business name, address and phone number on directories and other sites, links from local sites that mention the business, and a website that other sites link to because it is useful. Prominence is also where age helps: a business that has been trading for years, with reviews spread across that time, looks more established than a business created last month — which is one more reason to start the profile and the review habit early, even before you need the rankings. Think of prominence as a reputation that Google can see: every genuine review, every accurate listing, every local mention adds a little, and the businesses that win the Map Pack over time are the ones that have been adding a little, steadily, for months.

Getting Your Google Business Profile Complete

Before anything else, the profile has to be complete — because an incomplete profile loses on relevance and on prominence at the same time. Work through the checklist: business name exactly as it appears on your shop front and your website, with no extra keywords stuffed into it; the correct primary category and the additional categories you genuinely serve; the full services list with descriptions; an accurate address or service area; correct opening hours, including special hours for holidays; a phone number that is answered; a website link; and a business description written in plain words. Then the visual part: a logo, a cover photo, and real photos of the business — the premises, the team, the work, the products. Profiles with photos get more engagement than profiles without them, and photos are also evidence that the business is real and active. Add photos regularly, not once: a new photo every month or two keeps the profile looking alive. Fill in the questions and answers section too — add the questions customers actually ask, with honest answers, because those answers are visible to searchers and matchable to searches. And keep everything up to date: hours that are wrong at Christmas, a phone number that changed, a service you no longer offer — each one is a small loss of trust, and a customer who turns up to a closed shop will not come back. Reviews: The Steady Flow That Keeps You Visible

Reviews are the most visible part of prominence, and they are also the part most small businesses leave to chance. A profile with a steady flow of recent, genuine reviews looks active and trusted; a profile whose newest review is two years old looks abandoned, even if the business is busy. The routine is simple: ask happy customers, at the right time — a day or two after the job is done, when the experience is fresh — and make it easy with your Google review link, sent by text, email or a card. Ask everyone who had a good experience, as a matter of routine, not just your favourite customers. Reply to every review you receive: a short, specific thank-you for the good ones, and a calm, helpful reply for the bad ones, addressing the issue and offering to make it right offline. Replies show future customers that the business listens, and they give you a public chance to fix a problem. Never buy reviews, never offer payment or discounts for them, and never post fake ones — Google removes them, and a business caught doing it loses trust it cannot buy back. And do not chase a single big push for reviews and then stop: a steady trickle — a few reviews every month, replied to promptly — is better than fifty reviews in one week followed by silence for a year. Consistency is what both Google and customers read as a sign of a business that is still going strong. Citations: Consistent Name, Address and Phone

A citation is a mention of your business — name, address and phone number — on another website: a directory, a review site, a local business listing, a chamber of commerce page. Citations matter because they confirm to Google that the business is real and that its details are what the profile says they are. The rule is consistency: the name, address and phone number should be written the same way everywhere. A business listed as "Velrixa Studio" on its profile, "Velrixa Studios" on one directory and "Velrixa" on another, with a different phone format on each, sends mixed signals — and mixed signals weaken prominence. Start with the big, trusted directories and review sites for your country, and make sure each listing matches the profile exactly. Then the local ones: your town's business directory, your trade association, local news sites that have mentioned you. Check them once a quarter, because directories change, old phone numbers resurface, and a listing created years ago by someone else can still be live with wrong details. Remove or correct duplicates where you can, and claim listings that belong to you so you control them. Do not pay for hundreds of low-quality directory listings: a few accurate listings on trusted sites are worth far more than a hundred on sites nobody visits. And keep the website consistent too — the same name, address and phone number, on every page, usually in the footer and on the contact page, so that Google and customers always find the same details.

Local Content on Your Website

Your website supports the Map Pack in two ways: it adds relevance, because its pages describe your services and the areas you serve in the words searchers use, and it adds prominence, because it is the thing other sites link to when they mention you. The most useful pages are the service pages: one page per service, written in the customer's words, with what the service is, who it is for, what is included, and the areas you offer it in. A page about one service in one area — "emergency plumbing in Manchester", for example — is far more relevant to that search than a single page listing everything you do. Add a page for each area you serve, if you genuinely serve it, describing the service there and any local detail that matters. Then the supporting content: guides that answer the questions your customers actually ask, like this blog, which earns links and shows Google that the business is an authority in its field. Keep the business details consistent on every page — the same name, address and phone number — and link from the website to the Business Profile and back, so the two reinforce each other. And keep the website fast and usable on a phone, because a searcher who taps from the Map Pack to a slow, unreadable website will go back and call the next business instead. What to Do When You Are Stuck Just Outside the Pack

Most businesses that work on the Map Pack spend a while in fourth or fifth place — visible in the full list of results, but not in the three that get the calls. When you are stuck there, do not change everything at once. First, check the basics again: is the profile complete, are the categories right, are the hours and phone number correct, and is there a photo newer than six months? Second, compare yourself honestly with the three businesses in the Pack for your main search. How many reviews do they have, how recent are they, and do they reply to them? Are their categories more specific than yours? Do they have service pages on their websites for the area and the service? The gap is usually visible in that comparison, and it tells you what to do next. Third, look at the reviews: if the businesses in the Pack have a steady flow of recent reviews and yours has not had one in months, the review routine is the next job — not a new website. Fourth, check the search itself: search from different places in your area, and for different versions of the service, because the Pack changes with the searcher's location and the words they use. You may already be in the Pack for searches made from one part of town and not from another, which means the work is about the areas where you are not showing. And give it time. Changes to a profile — a new category, a new service, a run of new reviews — do not change the Pack overnight. Wait a month, check again from the same places, and judge by the trend: are you appearing for more searches, in more places, than you were last month? If the answer is yes, keep going. The businesses that win the Map Pack are rarely the ones that did one clever thing; they are the ones that kept the profile complete, the reviews flowing and the details consistent, month after month, while their competitors let theirs drift.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Google Map Pack?

The Map Pack is the block of three local business listings, shown with a map, that appears at the top of Google search results when someone searches for a local service — for example, "plumber near me" or "dentist in Lahore". The three businesses shown are chosen by Google from the businesses with Google Business Profiles in the area, and the Map Pack usually sits above the normal organic results, so it gets a large share of the clicks and calls.

How long does it take to rank in the Map Pack?

There is no fixed timeline. A business in a quiet area with few competitors and a complete profile can appear in the Map Pack within weeks of setting up and gathering its first reviews. In a competitive city or category, it can take months of steady work — keeping the profile complete, earning reviews regularly, keeping citations consistent, and publishing local content. The honest answer is that it takes as long as it takes to build a stronger local presence than the businesses you are competing with.

Can I rank in the Map Pack without a physical shop?

Yes, if you are a service-area business — a tradesperson, a cleaner, a designer who visits clients — you can set up a Google Business Profile with a service area instead of a public address. Google hides your home address and shows the areas you serve. What matters is that the business is real, serves that area, and has a complete profile with reviews and consistent information elsewhere on the web.

Do reviews really affect Map Pack rankings?

Reviews are one of the signals Google uses when choosing which businesses to show in local results. A profile with a steady flow of recent, genuine reviews, a healthy average rating, and replies from the owner looks active and reliable. Reviews also affect the click: when two businesses appear side by side in the Map Pack, the one with more reviews and a higher rating usually gets the call. No one can promise a specific ranking from reviews alone, but a business that ignores them is at a disadvantage.
