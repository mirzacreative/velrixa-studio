Local SEO Audit Checklist for Small Businesses

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

A local SEO audit checks the six things that decide whether nearby customers find you: your Google Business Profile, the consistency of your business name, address and phone number across the web, your reviews, your local keywords and citations, and the local signals on your website. Work through this checklist once a quarter, score each item pass or fail, and fix the failures in order — profile first, then name-address-phone consistency, then reviews, then the website. Most small businesses find the same problems: an incomplete profile, an old phone number on a directory somewhere, and no system for asking happy customers for reviews.

When a customer searches for a plumber, a dentist, a cafe or a designer "near me", Google shows a small set of local businesses — and most searchers call one of the first three. If your business is not in that set, the customer never sees you, no matter how good your work is. A local SEO audit is how you find out why. It is a fixed checklist you run against your Google Business Profile, your listings across the web, your reviews, and your website, so you can see exactly what is holding your local visibility back instead of guessing. This guide walks through the whole audit in order, with the fix for every problem it finds. If you want the strategy behind the checklist, start with our guide to local SEO strategy for small businesses — this post is the hands-on audit that puts that strategy into practice.

In this guide:
  • Google Business Profile audit — completeness, categories, photos and posts
  • NAP consistency — name, address and phone number across the web
  • Reviews audit — quantity, rating, recency and replies
  • Citations and directories — where you are listed, and where you are wrong
  • Website on-page checks — local keywords, location pages and schema
  • Technical and mobile checks, and how to score and fix it all

1. Google Business Profile: The Audit That Matters Most

Your Google Business Profile is the single most important local SEO asset you own, so audit it first. Open it and check every field. Is the business name exactly right — your real trading name, with no extra keywords stuffed in, because Google penalises that? Is the primary category the most accurate one for what you actually do, and have you added the useful secondary categories? Is the address correct and formatted the way Google shows it, are the opening hours right including holidays, and is the phone number the one you actually answer? Is the website link correct, and does the description describe your services in plain words with your main local keywords? Then check the visuals: do you have at least ten recent photos of the business, the team, the work and the premises, and is the logo current? Check the features too: are services, products or a menu filled in where they apply, are questions and answers monitored, and have you posted anything in the last month — an offer, an update, a photo? An empty profile looks abandoned to Google and to customers alike. Finally, check the profile on a phone, because that is how most local searchers see it: does the call button work, does the map pin sit on the right building, and does the profile answer the three questions a searcher has — what you do, where you are, and whether you are open now?

2. NAP Consistency: Name, Address and Phone Number

NAP stands for name, address and phone number, and consistency is the rule: the exact same details, written the exact same way, everywhere your business appears online. Search for your business name and your phone number separately and write down every listing you find — Google, Apple Maps, Bing Places, Yelp, Facebook, industry directories, local chamber listings, and any old directory you signed up for years ago. Check each one for differences: "Smith Plumbing" on one site and "Smith Plumbing LLC" on another, a street address written two different ways, an old mobile number still showing on a directory you forgot about, or a postcode with a typo. Every mismatch weakens Google's confidence that your listings are the same business. The fix is unglamorous but effective: pick one exact version of your name, address and phone number — the version on your Google Business Profile — and correct every other listing to match it, character for character. Pay special attention to old listings from a previous address or phone number, because those are the ones most businesses forget. This is the audit step most small businesses skip, and it is often the one that makes the biggest difference.

3. Reviews: Quantity, Rating, Recency and Replies

Reviews affect both your local ranking and whether a searcher chooses you over the business next door, so audit them properly. First, count: how many reviews do you have on Google, and how does that compare with the top three competitors in your area? A business with 12 reviews competes poorly against one with 120, even if the work is just as good. Second, check the average rating and read the recent ones — a run of one-star reviews about the same issue is a business problem, not a marketing problem, and it needs fixing at the source. Third, check recency: if your newest review is six months old, the profile looks quiet, and fresh reviews matter more than old ones. Fourth, check your replies. Have you replied to every review, including the negative ones, politely and specifically? Replies show Google and customers that the business is active and takes feedback seriously, and a calm, helpful reply to a bad review can turn a warning into a recommendation. The audit question to end with is the practical one: do you have a system for asking happy customers for a review — a short message after the job, a card at the till, a link in the receipt email? If the answer is no, that system is the most valuable fix in this whole checklist, because reviews only grow when someone asks for them.

4. Citations and Directories: Where You Are Listed

Citations are mentions of your business name, address and phone number on other websites, and they support your local ranking when they are accurate — and hurt it when they are wrong. List every directory and platform where your business appears: the big ones like Yelp, Apple Maps and Bing Places, industry directories for your trade, local business directories, and your social media profiles. Check each one for the NAP errors from step two, and for outdated information such as old opening hours, a retired service, or a link to a dead page. Check whether your main competitors are listed somewhere you are not — a local chamber directory, an industry association, a "best of" list — because those are citations you can claim too. Remove or correct duplicates, especially duplicate Google profiles, which confuse customers and split your reviews. Do not buy bulk citation packages from services that promise hundreds of listings overnight: a handful of accurate listings on real, relevant directories beats a hundred listings on sites nobody visits, and the cleanup from a bad bulk submission can take longer than doing it properly by hand.

5. Website On-Page Checks: Local Keywords and Location Signals

Your website has to tell Google where you are and what you do, in words a searcher would actually type. Check the homepage first: does the title tag include your main service and your city or area, and does the first paragraph say where you are based and who you serve? Check your service pages: each one should target one service in one area, with the place name used naturally in the heading and the copy, not stuffed into every sentence. If you serve several towns, check whether each one has its own page with genuinely local content — local landmarks, local examples, local photos — rather than the same page with the town name swapped. Check the contact page: is the address, phone number and opening hours shown clearly, and does it match the Google Business Profile exactly? Check the footer: does it carry the same NAP details on every page? Check for local business schema markup, which helps search engines understand your business type, address and hours — and if you are unsure whether it is present, ask your web developer to check, because it is a small addition with a real effect. 6. Technical, Mobile and Tracking Checks

Finish the audit with the technical basics. Open your site on a phone and check the three things local searchers do most: can they tap the phone number to call, can they tap the address to open directions, and can they find your opening hours in under ten seconds? Check the loading speed on mobile, because local searchers are often on the move and a slow page loses them before it loads. Check that the site works over HTTPS and that there are no broken links on the homepage, the contact page or the location pages. Check your analytics for the local signals that matter: how many visitors arrive from local searches, which pages they land on, and how many of them tap to call or submit the contact form. If you cannot see those numbers, set up call tracking and form tracking before the next audit, because an audit you cannot measure is a guess. Finally, check your competitors the same way: open the top three local results for your main keyword and note what they have that you do not — more reviews, better photos, a fuller profile, a location page you lack. That list is your fix list for the next quarter.

Score Your Audit and Fix in the Right Order

Score each section as you go, one point per pass: profile complete, NAP consistent, reviews healthy, citations clean, website local signals present, and technical basics working. A section that passes is done — leave it alone and move on. Fix the failures in this order, because the order is the order of impact: first the Google Business Profile, because it is what searchers see; then NAP consistency, because it is what Google trusts; then reviews, because they decide the click; then citations; then the website; then the technical fixes. Do not try to fix everything in one week. Fix the profile and the NAP errors first, wait two to four weeks, and check whether your local visibility and calls have moved. Then fix the next section. Repeat the full audit every three months, and check the profile weekly for new reviews, questions and photo requests. Local SEO is not a project you finish — it is a habit you keep, and the audit is the habit that keeps it honest. Frequently Asked Questions

How often should a small business audit its local SEO?

Run a full local SEO audit every three months, and check your Google Business Profile weekly. Local search changes when competitors update their profiles, when new reviews arrive, and when Google changes how the local results look. A quarterly audit catches inconsistent business details, missing categories and stale photos before they cost you calls.

What is the most common local SEO problem an audit finds?

Inconsistent business name, address and phone number across the web — usually called NAP inconsistency. A business listed as "Smith Plumbing" on Google, "Smith Plumbing LLC" on Yelp, and with an old phone number on a directory, sends Google mixed signals about which listing is real. Fixing the details to match exactly everywhere is often the highest-impact step in the whole audit.

Do reviews really affect local search rankings?

Yes. Review quantity, average rating and how recently reviews were left all play a part in how a business shows up in local results, and they strongly affect whether a searcher clicks and calls. Just as important is replying to reviews — including negative ones — because replies show Google and customers that the business is active and responsive.

How long does a local SEO audit take?

About two to three hours for a small business with one location: one hour for the Google Business Profile and reviews, one hour for NAP and citations, and one hour for the website, local content and technical checks. The fixes take longer, so score everything first and fix the highest-impact problems — profile completeness, NAP consistency and reviews — before the rest.
