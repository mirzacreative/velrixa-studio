Local SEO for Lawyers: Get More Local Clients

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

Most people who need a lawyer do not start by asking a friend, and they do not start on a law firm's website. They start on Google, usually on a phone, usually because something has happened that worries them — a relationship ending, an accident, an arrest, a house purchase, a dispute at work, a death in the family — and they type the problem and the place together: "family lawyer near me", "personal injury solicitor", "conveyancing solicitor", followed by the town or neighbourhood. The firms they see first are the ones with a complete Google Business Profile, recent reviews from real clients, a website that answers the questions a worried person asks, and pages for the practice areas people actually search for, written in plain words. This guide builds that presence, piece by piece, for a law firm: the profile, the reviews, the website, the practice-area pages, the local content, and the routine that keeps it all working, month after month. Nothing here promises a ranking or a number of clients. What it promises is a firm that is easy to find, easy to trust, and easy to contact — and for work that decides whether a person's family, home, livelihood or liberty is protected, that is the work that matters. A person searching for a lawyer is not browsing. They are deciding, often quickly, and often while they are worried, upset, or under pressure. The person whose marriage is ending wants a lawyer who will listen, explain the process plainly, and protect what matters to them. The person injured in an accident wants a lawyer who will take the case seriously, explain the next steps, and keep them informed. The person buying a house wants a lawyer who will handle the paperwork carefully, meet the deadlines, and explain what is happening in plain words. The person facing a criminal charge wants a lawyer who will act quickly, speak plainly, and be reachable. Each of those searchers is comparing a small number of firms, and they are comparing them on the evidence Google shows: the profile, the reviews, the photographs, and the first lines of the website. A firm that looks complete, recent and human will win that comparison more often than a firm that looks empty, out of date, or like every other firm in the area. Local SEO for lawyers is therefore not a technical exercise. It is the work of making a firm's real quality visible to a stranger, in the few seconds that stranger spends deciding — and for legal work, quality means listening first, plain dealing second, and reliability third. The profile must say the right things. The reviews must show real clients, recently, saying what the firm is actually good at. The website must answer the questions a prospective client asks before they call: can you help with my kind of problem, what happens first, what will it cost, how do I reach you, and will what I tell you stay private? And the firm must keep doing all of it, week after week, because a profile that was perfect last year and has not changed since tells a searcher the firm may not be paying attention now. This guide works through each piece in the order a law firm should build it, starting with the profile that every local search shows first.

How Prospective Clients Actually Search — and What They Compare

Legal searches fall into three groups, and a firm needs to be ready for all three, because each group is a different kind of client. The first group is the urgent search: an arrest, an accident, an eviction notice, a court date that is close, or a family crisis that cannot wait. The client wants help soon, close by, and from a firm that will answer the phone, listen, and act. They search on a phone, they look at the opening hours, the phone number and the distance first, and they call the firm that looks reachable and ready. Speed and reassurance come first in this group, and the website and the profile must say plainly how to reach the firm outside opening hours, what to do first, and what will happen on the first call, in careful terms that do not ask the client to share anything sensitive in a public place. The second group is the problem search: a separation that may lead to divorce, a dispute with an employer, a will that needs updating, a house purchase that needs a conveyancer, or a business disagreement that needs advice. The client is not in an emergency, but they have decided to get help, and they are comparing firms on the service, the approach, the experience, and the cost. They search the problem and the place together, they read the practice-area page, and they look at the reviews for clients who had the same problem. The third group is the planned-work search: making a will, planning for the future, setting up a business, or reviewing contracts before signing. The client is comparing several firms, they are in no hurry, and they choose on trust — reviews, the feel of the website, whether the firm seems careful, knowledgeable and easy to deal with, and whether the process is explained in plain words. All three groups compare the same evidence, in the same order: the profile's completeness, the recency and tone of the reviews, the photographs, and the website's first lines. A firm that serves all three groups will have a profile that shows it can handle urgent work safely, practice-area pages that answer the problem questions, and planned-work pages that explain the process, the documents needed, and the way costs are worked out. A firm that only serves one group — only urgent work, or only planned work — will miss the clients in the other two, and will look incomplete to a searcher who wanted the other kind of help. Build for all three, and let each kind of client find the page that was written for them.

Your Google Business Profile: The Front Door of the Firm

The Google Business Profile is the first thing a local searcher sees, and for many prospective clients it is the only thing they will read before calling. It must therefore be complete, accurate and human. Start with the basics, and check them against the firm itself: the exact firm name, as it appears on the office and on the website; the phone number that is answered during the hours shown; the office address as it really is, including how a client reaches it; the areas served, named honestly as the places the firm will actually take work from; and the opening hours as they really are, including any out-of-hours cover and any days the firm closes. A single wrong detail — a phone number that rings unanswered, hours that say open when nobody is answering, an area listed that the firm will not serve — will lose a client who was ready to call, and will teach the searcher not to trust the profile next time. Then add the practice areas the firm offers, using the words clients use. A client searches for "family lawyer", "divorce solicitor", "personal injury lawyer", "conveyancing solicitor", "wills and probate" and "employment lawyer", not for the formal names of the legal procedures, so the profile should carry both: the client words first, the formal terms where they help. Add the details that answer a client's practical questions: whether an initial consultation is offered and how it works, how the firm charges, whether appointments can be made by phone, by message, or both, and how to book. State any qualifications, memberships and accreditations honestly and only where they are genuinely held, and keep them up to date, because a claim that cannot be shown when asked will damage trust far more than saying nothing. Advertising and professional-conduct rules for lawyers vary by jurisdiction, and a firm should check the rules that apply to it before publishing any claim about its services, its results, or its standing — this guide states no rule as applying everywhere, and a claim that is acceptable in one place may not be acceptable in another. Write the firm description in plain words, saying who the firm serves, what it is known for, and what a new client can expect when they first make contact — including that their enquiry will be treated privately — rather than a list of credentials a client cannot judge. The profile is not a one-time job. Check it every month, update the hours before every holiday, and add a new practice area the week it is offered, because a profile that is out of date is worse than a profile that was never finished — it tells the searcher the firm is not paying attention.

Reviews: The Trust Signal No Advert Can Buy

For a law firm, reviews are not a marketing extra. They are the evidence a stranger uses to decide whether to trust a firm with a matter that may affect their family, their home, their livelihood or their liberty, and they are the first thing most searchers read after the profile itself. A firm with a steady flow of recent, specific reviews — from clients who name the kind of matter, how they were treated, and how the process was explained — will be chosen over a firm with more reviews that are older, vaguer, or all from the same month. The way to earn reviews is simple, and it is the way most firms fail to do: ask at the right moment, in person, and make it easy. The right moment is when a matter has concluded well and the client is satisfied — the agreement is signed, the purchase has completed, the case has been resolved, the will is in place. A short, honest request from the lawyer or the team member who handled the matter, with a link the client can use on their phone before they leave the office, will bring in more reviews than any message sent weeks later, when the matter is behind them and the client is busy. Before starting any review routine, check the professional-conduct and advertising rules that apply to the firm, because those rules vary and this guide does not state them: the routine must fit the rules, and where a rule limits what a firm may do, the rule comes first. Never offer a reward for a review, never write a review for a client, and never ask only the happiest clients while avoiding the rest — a review profile that is all praise and no criticism will be read as managed, and a firm that only asks the happy clients will miss the honest feedback that makes the firm better. Reply to every review. Thank the client, by name where the review gives one, and say something specific about what they mentioned, without discussing their matter, so the reply reads as a real person answering a real client rather than a copied thank-you. Confidentiality matters here above all: never confirm that a reviewer was a client, never discuss the facts of a matter, and never share anything a client told the firm in confidence, however tempting it is to correct a reviewer who has described a matter inaccurately. Answer a critical review calmly, briefly, and without arguing or sharing client details: acknowledge the experience, apologise that it fell short, and invite the person to contact the firm directly so the matter can be looked into. A calm, human reply to a critical review does more for trust than a dozen five-star reviews, because it shows the searcher how the firm handles a problem — and every prospective client knows that problems happen, and wants to know the firm will handle theirs well. Our reviews guide covers the whole routine in detail, including the wording to use when asking and the way to handle the difficult reviews. Build the routine into the working week, so that asking for a review is as normal as closing a file, and the reviews will keep coming without a campaign or a push.

Photos That Show a Real Office and Real People

A person who has never used a law firm before is imagining the worst: a cold office, a lawyer who speaks in jargon, a bill they do not understand, and a matter that drags on without news. Photographs are the firm's chance to replace that imagination with reality, before the client ever calls. The photographs that do that job are not stock photographs of gavels and law books. They are real photographs of this firm: the office as a client will find it, the meeting room where a client will sit, the lawyers and the team who will handle the matter, photographed in good light and looking like themselves. A photograph of the lawyer who will take the first call, with a short introduction on the website, turns a stranger into a person, and a person is much easier to trust than a firm name. Photographs of the office matter because they answer the question every client asks first: will I feel welcome here, will I be listened to, and will this feel like a place where my problem will be taken seriously? Show the entrance, so a client who is nervous about a first visit can recognise it when they arrive. Show the meeting room, clean and private, because privacy is the first thing a client needs from a law firm. Show the team, not only the senior lawyers, because the client will deal with the whole team, and a team that looks approachable will be approached. Update the photographs when the firm changes — a new office, a new team member, a refurbished meeting room — because photographs from years ago will be noticed, and a firm whose photographs do not match its office will be doubted on everything else. Avoid the two photograph mistakes that damage professional profiles most: using no photographs at all, which leaves the client's imagination to fill the gap with the worst version, and using stock photographs, which tell the searcher the firm did not think its own office and people were worth showing. One honest set of photographs, taken in good light in the real office, with the team's permission, will do more for a nervous client than any amount of advertising, because it answers the question every client asks first: what will it actually be like when I walk in?

Your Website: The Questions Every Prospective Client Asks Before Calling

The website is where a searcher goes to decide whether to call, and it should answer, in its first lines, the questions every prospective client asks before they pick up the phone. The first question is whether the firm helps with their kind of problem. The homepage and the practice-area pages must name the problems plainly, in the client's words, so that a person facing a separation, an accident, a house purchase or a dispute can see at once that they are in the right place. The second question is what happens first. A first-time client does not know the process, and a website that explains it — the first call, the first meeting, what to bring, what will be asked, and what happens next — will be trusted more than a website that only lists services. The third question is cost. A client who cannot tell, even in general terms, how the firm charges will hesitate to call, and a website that explains the charging approach plainly — including whether an initial consultation is charged, how fees are worked out, and when the client will know the cost — will draw the clients who are ready to proceed. The website should not promise a fee it cannot keep, and should not hide the subject of cost until the client is already in the office. The fourth question is privacy. A client who is about to share something personal needs to know that their enquiry will be treated privately, that they should not share sensitive details in a public form or a public comment, and that the first conversation will be handled with care. Say so plainly, and design the contact forms so that they ask for only what is needed to arrange a first conversation. The fifth question is how to reach the firm: the phone number, the hours, the address, and the way to book, shown on every page, so that a client who has decided to call does not have to hunt for the number. Our profile posts guide covers the short updates that keep a firm visible between website visits, and the website itself should be checked regularly for the same faults as the profile: out-of-date hours, a practice area that is no longer offered, a team member who has left, or a photograph that no longer matches the office. A website that is accurate, plain, and easy to use will convert the searcher the profile attracted. A website that is vague, cluttered, or out of date will lose the same searcher in the last few seconds before the call.

Practice-Area Pages: One Page Per Area, Written for Clients

A practice-area page is a page written for one kind of client, about one kind of problem, and a firm needs one for every area it wants to be found for. A single services page that mentions every area in a sentence will not answer any client's questions, because a client facing a divorce, a client buying a house, and a client making a will have different questions, different worries, and different words for their problem. Each page should do the same job, in the same order. It should name the problem in the client's words, so the client knows at once they are in the right place. It should explain, in plain words, what the service covers and what it does not cover, so the client is not misled. It should explain the process, step by step, in the order the client will experience it, so that the unknown becomes manageable. It should say what the client should bring to a first meeting, and what the first meeting will cover, so that the client arrives prepared rather than anxious. It should explain, in general terms, how the cost is worked out, without promising a figure that cannot be kept. And it should say how to start: the phone number, the form, or the booking link, with the privacy note that belongs with it. The pages should be written in plain language throughout. Legal terms should be explained where they must be used, and avoided where a plain word will do, because a client who cannot understand the page will conclude that the firm will not explain things to them either. The pages should also be honest about limits: a firm that does not handle a particular kind of matter should not have a page that suggests it does, and a page should not promise an outcome that no lawyer can promise. Start with the areas the firm most wants to grow — usually the areas where the firm has its strongest experience and its happiest clients — write those pages first, and add the rest over time, including a page for each area's most common questions. One page that answers a client's questions for one area will serve the firm far better than one long services page that mentions every area in a sentence, and the set of pages, built over months, will become the firm's clearest statement of what it does and who it does it for.

Local Pages and Content: The Areas, the Community, the Real Firm

A law firm serves a place, and its website should show that it knows the place it serves. Local pages and local content do that job, provided they are written about the real firm in the real place, and not assembled from general phrases that could belong anywhere. A page for an area the firm serves should say, honestly, what the firm does there: the courts, the offices, or the institutions it deals with, the kinds of matter it handles for people in that area, and how a client from that area reaches the firm. A page that only repeats the area name beside the same text used for every other area will be recognised as filler by the searcher, and it will do the firm no good. The content that works is the content that could only have been written by this firm, about this place: a guide to a process that clients in the area ask about, written in the firm's own words; an explanation of a local procedure, written from the firm's own experience; or a note about a community the firm is part of, written because the firm is genuinely part of it. Community involvement should be shown, not claimed: a firm that supports a local organisation, that gives talks, that helps a local group, should say so plainly, with the detail that proves it, and a firm that does none of those things should not pretend otherwise. The same honesty applies to local news and changes. Where a change in the law, a change in a local procedure, or a change in the courts affects clients in the area, a short, plain explanation on the website, and a short post on the profile, will help the clients who need it and will show the firm as a firm that pays attention. Where no such change has happened, there is no need to invent one. Local content should be published steadily, not in a burst: one useful local page or post a month, kept accurate, will do more than ten pages published in a week and never updated. And every local page should end with the same next step — how to contact the firm, privately, about the matter the page discusses — because a page that informs without inviting contact has done only half its job.

Posts, Questions and Updates: Keeping the Profile Alive

A profile that never changes tells a searcher the firm may not be paying attention, and a profile that changes regularly tells the searcher the firm is working, active, and current. The way to keep a profile alive is a short post at least once a week, and an answer to every question a searcher asks on the profile. The posts should be useful, not promotional. A post that explains, in plain words, a question clients are asking this month will help the clients who read it and will show the firm as a firm that listens. A post that announces a new team member, a new office hour, or a new service the firm genuinely offers will inform the searchers who need to know. A post that only says the firm is the best, or that repeats the same message week after week, will be ignored, and a profile full of ignored posts looks worse than a profile with fewer, better posts. The questions on a profile deserve the same care as a first phone call. A searcher who asks a question on the profile is a prospective client deciding whether to call, and a prompt, plain, helpful answer — one that does not ask for sensitive details in public, and that invites the person to call or to use the private contact form — will do more than any post. A question left unanswered for weeks tells the searcher the firm does not answer questions, and that is the last impression a firm should give. Our profile posts guide covers the whole routine in detail, including the kinds of post that suit a professional firm and the wording that keeps a post useful rather than promotional. Build the routine into the working week: one post a week, one check of the questions a day, and one update to the profile whenever anything changes — hours, services, team, or address. The routine is small, and it is the smallness that makes it sustainable. A firm that keeps it up for a year will have a profile that looks alive in every month of that year, and a searcher who finds the firm in any of those months will find a firm that looks as if it is paying attention, because it is.

Consultations and Enquiries: Making the Next Step Effortless — and Private

The next step after finding a firm should be effortless, and it should be private. A prospective client who has decided to call should find the phone number at once, on every page, without hunting. A client who prefers to write should find a contact form that asks for only what is needed to arrange a first conversation — a name, a way to reply, and the kind of matter, in general terms — and that says plainly that the client should not share sensitive details in the form. A client who prefers to book should find a booking route that works, that confirms the appointment, and that says what to bring. The firm should answer enquiries promptly, and the promptness matters more than the polish of the reply: a short reply within the working day, saying when the client will hear more and what will happen next, will keep a client who might otherwise call the next firm on the list. The first conversation should be handled with the care the client's matter deserves. The person who answers should listen, should explain the process and the charging approach in plain words, should say what the client should bring, and should not promise an outcome that cannot be promised. Where the firm cannot help — the matter is outside its areas, or the firm has a conflict — the client should be told plainly and promptly, and, where possible, pointed towards the kind of help they need, because a client who is treated honestly when a firm cannot help will remember the firm well and may return with a matter the firm can help with. Confidentiality should be built into every step: the enquiry form, the phone manner, the meeting room, and the way the firm speaks about its work in public. A firm should never discuss a client's matter in a public reply, in a social media post, or in a review response, and should never use a client's story as marketing without the client's clear, informed permission, given in writing, for that specific use. Most firms will never need to use a client's story at all, and a website that explains its services well, with reviews that speak in general terms, will earn trust without ever telling a client's story. Make the next step easy, make it private, answer promptly, and the enquiries the profile and the website bring will become the consultations the firm wants.

Measuring What Matters: Enquiries, Consultations and the Right Clients

A law firm should judge its local presence by the enquiries and consultations it brings, not by a single position on a single day. The figures that matter are the ones the firm can see in its own records: how many enquiries arrived, from where, about which practice areas, and how many became consultations, and then matters. A firm that tracks those figures, month by month, will learn which pages bring the enquiries it wants, which areas are growing, and which posts or updates preceded a rise in calls. A firm that judges only by a position will be misled, because a position can move for reasons that have nothing to do with the firm, and a firm that chases a position will spend its time on the wrong work. The record should be simple: for each month, the number of enquiries, the practice areas they concerned, the source the client named — the profile, the website, a referral, or another route — and the outcome, in general terms. After a few months, that record will show the pattern: the areas that bring enquiries, the pages that do the work, and the times of year when enquiries rise or fall. The firm should also listen to what enquirers say. A client who says they called because a review described exactly their situation has told the firm what its reviews should keep doing. A client who says they could not tell from the website whether the firm handled their kind of matter has told the firm which page needs rewriting. A client who says they called because the profile answered their question has told the firm that answering questions matters. For the wider picture, read our guide to getting cited by AI search alongside this one, and judge the whole presence — profile, website, reviews, and answers — by the clients it brings over months, not by any one week's figures. The work is slow, and it should be: a firm's reputation is built matter by matter, review by review, and page by page, and a local presence that reflects that reputation honestly will keep bringing the right clients, month after month.

Mistakes That Cost Law Firms Clients

The mistakes worth naming are the ones firms repeat without noticing. A profile with the wrong phone number, the wrong hours, or an area the firm does not serve, so that a ready client is lost at the last step. A website that lists every practice area in a sentence and answers no client's questions, so that a searcher leaves for a firm whose page speaks to their problem. Reviews that are never asked for, so that a firm with happy clients looks, to a stranger, like a firm with no clients at all. Reviews that are answered by discussing the client's matter, so that a searcher concludes the firm cannot be trusted with confidences. Claims about results, standing, or expertise that the firm's own rules do not allow, published without checking the rules that apply. Photographs that are stock images, or years out of date, so that the office a client visits does not match the office the website showed. A contact form that asks for sensitive details in public, or a profile question answered by asking the searcher to share their story where anyone can read it. Posts that only promote the firm, week after week, so that the profile looks like advertising rather than a working firm. And a presence that was built once and never maintained, so that a searcher in any month after the first finds a firm that looks as if it stopped paying attention. Avoid those, keep the profile accurate, the reviews honest, the pages plain, and the routine steady, and the firm will be easy to find, easy to trust, and easy to contact — which is all that local SEO for a law firm can honestly promise, and all that a prospective client, worried and deciding quickly, is asking for.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does local SEO take for a law firm?

There is no fixed timescale, and no honest answer can promise one. The basics — a complete and accurate profile, correct details everywhere, a website that answers the questions prospective clients ask, and pages for the firm's main practice areas — can be put in place in a few weeks, and the effect of those basics is often visible quickly, because many firms have never done them properly. The deeper work — a steady flow of reviews, helpful local and practice-area pages, and a record of answering enquiries promptly, explaining the process plainly, and keeping clients informed — builds over months, and it is that record that keeps a firm visible over time. Judge the work by the enquiries and consultations it brings, over several months, rather than by a single week's position.

What should a law firm put on its Google Business Profile?

The exact firm name, the correct phone number, the office address as it really is, the areas served, the opening hours as they really are, the practice areas offered, and real photographs of the office, the team, and the meeting space where clients are welcomed. Add the practice areas and services clients search for — family law, personal injury, criminal defence, conveyancing, wills and probate, employment, and business law, where the firm genuinely offers them — using the words clients use, not only the legal terms. Keep the hours, the areas and the services up to date, answer the questions prospective clients ask on the profile, and post a short update at least once a week, so the profile looks like a firm that is working, active and paying attention.

How can a law firm get more Google reviews without breaking professional rules?

Ask at the right moment, in person, and make it easy — and check the professional-conduct and advertising rules that apply to the firm before starting, because those rules vary and this guide does not state them. The right moment is when a matter has concluded well and the client is satisfied: a short, honest request from the lawyer or the team member who handled the matter, with a link the client can use on their phone, will bring in more reviews than any message sent weeks later. Never offer a reward for a review, never write a review for a client, and never ask only the happiest clients while avoiding the rest. Reply to every review, thank the client without discussing their matter, and answer a critical review calmly, briefly, and without sharing any client details. A calm, human reply to a critical review does more for trust than a dozen five-star reviews.

Does a law firm need a page for every practice area?

A firm needs a page for every practice area it wants to be found for, written for the client who needs that help, because a single services page that mentions every area in a sentence will not answer any client's questions properly. A family-law page should answer the questions a person facing a separation asks. A conveyancing page should answer the questions a buyer or seller asks. A wills page should answer the questions a person planning ahead asks. Each page should explain, in plain words, what the service covers, how the process works, what the client should bring to a first meeting, and how to start. Start with the areas the firm most wants to grow, write those pages first, and add the rest over time. One page that answers a client's questions for one area will serve the firm far better than one long page that mentions every area briefly.
