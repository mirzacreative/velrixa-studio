Local SEO for Real Estate Agents: Get More Local Clients

By Velrixa Studio · Digital Growth Strategy

Key takeaways

Most people who need an estate agent do not start by asking a friend, and they do not start on an agency's website. They start on Google, usually on a phone, usually because something has changed — a move for work, a growing family, a separation, an inheritance, a decision to sell, or a first home to buy — and they type the need and the place together: "estate agent near me", "houses for sale", "flats to rent", or "property valuation", followed by the town or neighbourhood. The agents they see first are the ones with a complete Google Business Profile, recent reviews from real buyers and sellers, a website that answers the questions a mover asks, and pages for the areas and property types people actually search for, written in plain words. This guide builds that presence, piece by piece, for a real estate agent: the profile, the reviews, the website, the area pages, the listing content, and the routine that keeps it all working, month after month. Nothing here promises a ranking or a number of clients. What it promises is an agent who is easy to find, easy to trust, and easy to contact — and for work that decides where a person will live, and the largest purchase or sale most people will ever make, that is the work that matters. A person searching for an estate agent is not browsing. They are deciding, often quickly, and often while they are under pressure. The person who must move for work wants an agent who will listen, act quickly, and keep them informed. The person selling a family home wants an agent who will value it honestly, present it well, and explain the process plainly. The person buying a first home wants an agent who will explain each step, answer questions without jargon, and not rush them into a decision. The landlord with a property to let wants an agent who will find a suitable tenant, handle the paperwork carefully, and manage the property reliably. Each of those searchers is comparing a small number of agents, and they are comparing them on the evidence Google shows: the profile, the reviews, the photographs, and the first lines of the website. An agent who looks complete, recent and human will win that comparison more often than an agent who looks empty, out of date, or like every other agent in the area. Local SEO for real estate agents is therefore not a technical exercise. It is the work of making an agent's real quality visible to a stranger, in the few seconds that stranger spends deciding — and for property work, quality means local knowledge first, plain dealing second, and reliability third. The profile must say the right things. The reviews must show real buyers and sellers, recently, saying what the agent is actually good at. The website must answer the questions a prospective client asks before they call: do you cover my area, do you handle my kind of property, what happens first, what will it cost, how do I reach you, and will my details be kept private? And the agent must keep doing all of it, week after week, because a profile that was perfect last year and has not changed since tells a searcher the agent may not be paying attention now. This guide works through each piece in the order an agent should build it, starting with the profile that every local search shows first.

How Buyers and Sellers Actually Search — and What They Compare

Property searches fall into three groups, and an agent needs to be ready for all three, because each group is a different kind of client. The first group is the urgent search: a move that cannot wait, a sale that must complete by a date, a tenant who must be found quickly, or a purchase that depends on a chain. The client wants help soon, close by, and from an agent who will answer the phone, listen, and act. They search on a phone, they look at the opening hours, the phone number and the distance first, and they call the agent who looks reachable and ready. Speed and reassurance come first in this group, and the website and the profile must say plainly how to reach the agent outside opening hours, what to do first, and what will happen on the first call, in careful terms that do not ask the client to share anything sensitive in a public place. The second group is the comparing search: a seller comparing agents before choosing one to value and market a home, a buyer comparing agents in the area they want to move to, or a landlord comparing agents to manage a property. The client is not in an emergency, but they have decided to act, and they are comparing agents on local knowledge, presentation, fees, communication, and reviews. They search the service and the place together, they read the area page, and they look at the reviews for clients who had the same kind of move. The third group is the planning search: a buyer researching areas before moving, a seller thinking about moving next year, or an owner considering letting a property. The client is comparing several agents, they are in no hurry, and they choose on trust — reviews, the feel of the website, whether the agent seems knowledgeable about the area, careful with presentation, and easy to deal with, and whether the process is explained in plain words. All three groups compare the same evidence, in the same order: the profile's completeness, the recency and tone of the reviews, the photographs, and the website's first lines. An agent who serves all three groups will have a profile that shows it can handle urgent work safely, area pages that answer the comparing questions, and planning pages that explain the process, the costs, and the way valuations and viewings work. An agent who only serves one group — only urgent lettings, or only planned sales — will miss the clients in the other two, and will look incomplete to a searcher who wanted the other kind of help. Build for all three, and let each kind of client find the page that was written for them.

Your Google Business Profile: The Front Door of the Agency

The Google Business Profile is the first thing a local searcher sees, and for many prospective clients it is the only thing they will read before calling. It must therefore be complete, accurate and human. Start with the basics, and check them against the agency itself: the exact agency or agent name, as it appears on the office and on the website; the phone number that is answered during the hours shown; the office address as it really is, including how a client reaches it; the areas served, named honestly as the places the agent will actually take work from; and the opening hours as they really are, including any out-of-hours cover and any days the office closes. A single wrong detail — a phone number that rings unanswered, hours that say open when nobody is answering, an area listed that the agent will not serve — will lose a client who was ready to call, and will teach the searcher not to trust the profile next time. Then add the services the agent offers, using the words clients use. A client searches for "estate agent", "property valuation", "houses for sale", "flats to rent", "property management" and "letting agent", not for the formal names of the procedures, so the profile should carry both: the client words first, the formal terms where they help. Add the details that answer a client's practical questions: whether a valuation is offered and how it works, how the agent charges, whether appointments can be made by phone, by message, or both, and how to book a viewing. State any qualifications, memberships and accreditations honestly and only where they are genuinely held, and keep them up to date, because a claim that cannot be shown when asked will damage trust far more than saying nothing. Property advertising and agent conduct rules vary by jurisdiction, and an agent should check the rules that apply to them before publishing any claim about their services, their results, or their standing — this guide states no rule as applying everywhere, and a claim that is acceptable in one place may not be acceptable in another. Write the agency description in plain words, saying who the agent serves, what areas and property types the agent knows best, and what a new client can expect when they first make contact — including that their enquiry will be treated privately — rather than a list of credentials a client cannot judge. Our setup guide walks through the whole process step by step, and our Map Pack guide explains how the profile works alongside the website and the reviews in the local results. The profile is not a one-time job. Check it every month, update the hours before every holiday, and add a new area or service the week it is offered, because a profile that is out of date is worse than a profile that was never finished — it tells the searcher the agent is not paying attention.

Reviews: The Trust Signal No Advert Can Buy

For a real estate agent, reviews are not a marketing extra. They are the evidence a stranger uses to decide whether to trust an agent with the largest purchase or sale most people will ever make, and they are the first thing most searchers read after the profile itself. An agent with a steady flow of recent, specific reviews — from buyers and sellers who name the kind of move, how they were treated, and how the process was explained — will be chosen over an agent with more reviews that are older, vaguer, or all from the same month. The way to earn reviews is simple, and it is the way most agents fail to do: ask at the right moment, in person, and make it easy. The right moment is when a sale or letting has completed well and the client is satisfied — the keys have been handed over, the purchase has completed, the tenant has moved in. A short, honest request from the agent who handled the matter, with a link the client can use on their phone, will bring in more reviews than any message sent weeks later, when the move is behind them and the client is busy. Before starting any review routine, check the advertising and conduct rules that apply to the agent, because those rules vary and this guide does not state them: the routine must fit the rules, and where a rule limits what an agent may do, the rule comes first. Never offer a reward for a review, never write a review for a client, and never ask only the happiest clients while avoiding the rest — a review profile that is all praise and no criticism will be read as managed, and an agent who only asks the happy clients will miss the honest feedback that makes the agency better. Reply to every review. Thank the client, by name where the review gives one, and say something specific about what they mentioned, without discussing their property or their circumstances, so the reply reads as a real person answering a real client rather than a copied thank-you. Privacy matters here above all: never confirm details of a client's property, finances, or circumstances, never discuss the terms of a sale or letting, and never share anything a client told the agent in confidence, however tempting it is to correct a reviewer who has described a matter inaccurately. Answer a critical review calmly, briefly, and without arguing or sharing client details: acknowledge the experience, apologise that it fell short, and invite the person to contact the agency directly so the matter can be looked into. A calm, human reply to a critical review does more for trust than a dozen five-star reviews, because it shows the searcher how the agent handles a problem — and every prospective client knows that problems happen, and wants to know the agent will handle theirs well. Our reviews guide covers the whole routine in detail, including the wording to use when asking and the way to handle the difficult reviews. Build the routine into the working week, so that asking for a review is as normal as closing a file, and the reviews will keep coming without a campaign or a push.

Photos That Show a Real Office, a Real Team, and Real Places

A person who has never used an estate agent before is imagining the worst: an office that does not answer, an agent who speaks in jargon, a valuation that cannot be explained, and a move that drags on without news. Photographs are the agent's chance to replace that imagination with reality, before the client ever calls. The photographs that do that job are not stock photographs of keys and houses. They are real photographs of this agency: the office as a client will find it, the meeting space where a client will sit, the agents and the team who will handle the move, photographed in good light and looking like themselves, and the streets and places of the areas the agent serves. A photograph of the agent who will take the first call, with a short introduction on the website, turns a stranger into a person, and a person is much easier to trust than an agency name. Photographs of the office matter because they answer the question every client asks first: will I feel welcome here, will I be listened to, and will this feel like a place where my move will be taken seriously? Show the entrance, so a client who is nervous about a first visit can recognise it when they arrive. Show the meeting space, clean and private, because privacy is the first thing a client needs from an agent who will learn about their home, their finances, and their plans. Show the team, not only the senior agents, because the client will deal with the whole team, and a team that looks approachable will be approached. Show the area, too: the streets, the schools, the parks, and the places that make each neighbourhood what it is, photographed honestly, because a buyer choosing an area wants to see it as it really is, not as a brochure imagines it. Update the photographs when the agency changes — a new office, a new team member, a refurbished meeting space — because photographs from years ago will be noticed, and an agency whose photographs do not match its office will be doubted on everything else. Avoid the two photograph mistakes that damage professional profiles most: using no photographs at all, which leaves the client's imagination to fill the gap with the worst version, and using stock photographs, which tell the searcher the agency did not think its own office, people, and places were worth showing. One honest set of photographs, taken in good light in the real office and the real area, with the team's permission, will do more for a nervous client than any amount of advertising, because it answers the question every client asks first: what will it actually be like when I walk in, and do these people know my area?

Your Website: The Questions Every Buyer and Seller Asks Before Calling

The website is where a searcher goes to decide whether to call, and it should answer, in its first lines, the questions every buyer and seller asks before they pick up the phone. The first question is whether the agent covers their area and handles their kind of property. The homepage and the area pages must name the areas and property types plainly, in the client's words, so that a person selling a family house, buying a first flat, or looking for a rental can see at once that they are in the right place. The second question is what happens first. A first-time seller does not know the process, and a website that explains it — the valuation, the marketing, the viewings, the offers, and the steps to completion — will be trusted more than a website that only lists services. A first-time buyer needs the same: the steps from viewing to offer to completion, explained in plain words. The third question is cost. A client who cannot tell, even in general terms, how the agent charges will hesitate to call, and a website that explains the charging approach plainly — including how valuation and marketing are handled, and when the client will know the cost — will draw the clients who are ready to proceed. The website should not promise a fee it cannot keep, and should not hide the subject of cost until the client is already in the office. The fourth question is privacy. A client who is about to share something personal — their home, their plans, their finances — needs to know that their enquiry will be treated privately, that they should not share sensitive details in a public form or a public comment, and that the first conversation will be handled with care. Say so plainly, and design the contact forms so that they ask for only what is needed to arrange a first conversation. The fifth question is how to reach the agent: the phone number, the hours, the address, and the way to book a valuation or a viewing, shown on every page, so that a client who has decided to call does not have to hunt for the number. Our profile posts guide covers the short updates that keep an agent visible between website visits, and the website itself should be checked regularly for the same faults as the profile: out-of-date hours, an area that is no longer covered, a team member who has left, a property type no longer handled, or a photograph that no longer matches the office. A website that is accurate, plain, and easy to use will convert the searcher the profile attracted. A website that is vague, cluttered, or out of date will lose the same searcher in the last few seconds before the call.

Area Pages: One Page Per Area, Written for Local Movers

An area page is a page written for one kind of client, in one place, and an agent needs one for every area they want to be found for. A single areas page that mentions every neighbourhood in a sentence will not answer any client's questions, because a buyer looking in one neighbourhood, a seller in another, and a landlord in a third have different questions, different worries, and different words for their move. Each page should do the same job, in the same order. It should name the area in the client's words, so the client knows at once they are in the right place. It should describe the area honestly, in the agent's own words, from the agent's own knowledge: the kinds of property, the streets and pockets within the area, the schools, transport, parks, and places that matter to movers, and the kinds of buyer and seller the area suits. It should explain, in plain words, what the agent does in that area: the sales and lettings handled there, the valuations offered, and how a client from that area reaches the agent. It should answer the questions movers ask about that area: what kinds of property are available, what the buying and selling process looks like there, and what a new client should do first. And it should say how to start: the phone number, the form, or the booking link, with the privacy note that belongs with it. The pages should be written in plain language throughout, and they should be honest about limits: an agent who does not cover an area should not have a page that suggests they do, and a page should not promise a result that no agent can promise. Start with the areas the agent most wants to grow — usually the areas where the agent has its strongest knowledge and its happiest clients — write those pages first, and add the rest over time, including a page for each area's most common questions. One page that answers a client's questions for one area will serve the agent far better than one long areas page that mentions every neighbourhood in a sentence, and the set of pages, built over months, will become the agent's clearest statement of the places it knows and the movers it serves.

Listing Pages and Content: The Property, the Area, the Process

A listing is the part of an agent's website that a buyer meets most directly, and it should be built to answer the buyer's questions, not only to show the property. The listing should state the facts plainly: the property type, the number of rooms, the size where it can be stated honestly, the features that matter to a buyer, and the price or rental figure as it stands, without wording that promises more than the property offers. The photographs should show the property as it really is, in good light, in an order that lets a buyer walk through it in their mind: the outside, the rooms a buyer will use most, and the details that make the property what it is. A listing whose photographs hide a room, hide a fault, or show a property that no longer looks that way will be distrusted when the buyer views it in person, and a buyer who feels misled at a viewing will not trust the agent again. The listing should also say, plainly, what the area offers, linking to the area page, so that a buyer who likes the property can learn about the neighbourhood without searching elsewhere. And it should say what happens next: how to book a viewing, what to bring, and what the agent will do after the viewing. Beyond individual listings, the website should carry the content that buyers and sellers search for when they are not yet ready to view: a plain guide to buying a first home, a plain guide to selling, a plain guide to valuations, and a plain guide to letting, each written in the agent's own words, from the agent's own experience, and each ending with the same next step — how to contact the agent, privately, about the matter the page discusses. Property advertising rules vary, and an agent should check the rules that apply before publishing any listing or claim, including the wording of prices, features, and availability. A listing that is accurate, complete, and easy to act on will draw the viewers the property deserves. A listing that is vague, exaggerated, or out of date will draw viewers who leave disappointed, and will waste the viewings that a careful listing would have earned.

Posts, Questions and Updates: Keeping the Profile Alive

A profile that never changes tells a searcher the agent may not be paying attention, and a profile that changes regularly tells the searcher the agent is working, active, and current. The way to keep a profile alive is a short post at least once a week, and an answer to every question a searcher asks on the profile. The posts should be useful, not promotional. A post that explains, in plain words, a question buyers or sellers are asking this month will help the clients who read it and will show the agent as an agent that listens. A post that announces a new property, a new area covered, or a new service the agent genuinely offers will inform the searchers who need to know, provided the property and area details are accurate and up to date. A post that only says the agent is the best, or that repeats the same message week after week, will be ignored, and a profile full of ignored posts looks worse than a profile with fewer, better posts. The questions on a profile deserve the same care as a first phone call. A searcher who asks a question on the profile is a prospective client deciding whether to call, and a prompt, plain, helpful answer — one that does not ask for sensitive details in public, and that invites the person to call or to use the private contact form — will do more than any post. A question left unanswered for weeks tells the searcher the agent does not answer questions, and that is the last impression an agent should give. Our profile posts guide covers the whole routine in detail, including the kinds of post that suit a professional agency and the wording that keeps a post useful rather than promotional. Build the routine into the working week: one post a week, one check of the questions a day, and one update to the profile whenever anything changes — hours, areas, services, team, or address. The routine is small, and it is the smallness that makes it sustainable. An agent that keeps it up for a year will have a profile that looks alive in every month of that year, and a searcher who finds the agent in any of those months will find an agent that looks as if it is paying attention, because it is.

Viewings and Enquiries: Making the Next Step Effortless — and Private

The next step after finding an agent should be effortless, and it should be private. A prospective client who has decided to call should find the phone number at once, on every page, without hunting. A client who prefers to write should find a contact form that asks for only what is needed to arrange a first conversation — a name, a way to reply, and the kind of move, in general terms — and that says plainly that the client should not share sensitive details in the form. A client who prefers to book should find a booking route that works, that confirms the valuation or viewing, and that says what to bring. The agent should answer enquiries promptly, and the promptness matters more than the polish of the reply: a short reply within the working day, saying when the client will hear more and what will happen next, will keep a client who might otherwise call the next agent on the list. The first conversation should be handled with the care the client's move deserves. The person who answers should listen, should explain the process and the charging approach in plain words, should say what the client should bring, and should not promise an outcome that cannot be promised. Where the agent cannot help — the area is outside the areas covered, or the property type is not handled — the client should be told plainly and promptly, and, where possible, pointed towards the kind of help they need, because a client who is treated honestly when an agent cannot help will remember the agency well and may return with a move the agent can help with. Confidentiality should be built into every step: the enquiry form, the phone manner, the meeting space, and the way the agency speaks about its work in public. An agent should never discuss a client's property, finances, or circumstances in a public reply, in a social media post, or in a review response, and should never use a client's story as marketing without the client's clear, informed permission, given in writing, for that specific use. Most agencies will never need to use a client's story at all, and a website that explains its services well, with reviews that speak in general terms, will earn trust without ever telling a client's story. Make the next step easy, make it private, answer promptly, and the enquiries the profile and the website bring will become the viewings and valuations the agent wants.

Measuring What Matters: Enquiries, Viewings and the Right Clients

An agent should judge their local presence by the enquiries and viewings it brings, not by a single position on a single day. The figures that matter are the ones the agency can see in its own records: how many enquiries arrived, from where, about which areas and property types, and how many became valuations, viewings, and then sales or lettings. An agency that tracks those figures, month by month, will learn which pages bring the enquiries it wants, which areas are growing, and which posts or updates preceded a rise in calls. An agency that judges only by a position will be misled, because a position can move for reasons that have nothing to do with the agency, and an agent that chases a position will spend its time on the wrong work. The record should be simple: for each month, the number of enquiries, the areas and property types they concerned, the source the client named — the profile, the website, a referral, or another route — and the outcome, in general terms. After a few months, that record will show the pattern: the areas that bring enquiries, the pages that do the work, and the times of year when enquiries rise or fall. The agency should also listen to what enquirers say. A client who says they called because a review described exactly their situation has told the agency what its reviews should keep doing. A client who says they could not tell from the website whether the agent covered their area has told the agency which page needs rewriting. A client who says they called because the profile answered their question has told the agency that answering questions matters. For the wider picture, read our guide to getting cited by AI search and our backlinks guide alongside this one, and judge the whole presence — profile, website, reviews, and answers — by the clients it brings over months, not by any one week's figures. The work is slow, and it should be: an agency's reputation is built move by move, review by review, and page by page, and a local presence that reflects that reputation honestly will keep bringing the right clients, month after month.

Mistakes That Cost Real Estate Agents Clients

The mistakes worth naming are the ones agents repeat without noticing. A profile with the wrong phone number, the wrong hours, or an area the agent does not cover, so that a ready client is lost at the last step. A website that lists every area in a sentence and answers no client's questions, so that a searcher leaves for an agent whose page speaks to their move. Reviews that are never asked for, so that an agent with happy clients looks, to a stranger, like an agent with no clients at all. Reviews that are answered by discussing the client's property or circumstances, so that a searcher concludes the agent cannot be trusted with confidences. Claims about results, standing, or expertise that the agent's own rules do not allow, published without checking the rules that apply. Photographs that are stock images, or years out of date, so that the office a client visits does not match the office the website showed. A contact form that asks for sensitive details in public, or a profile question answered by asking the searcher to share their story where anyone can read it. Posts that only promote the agency, week after week, so that the profile looks like advertising rather than a working agency. Listings that are out of date, exaggerated, or missing the facts a buyer needs, so that viewings are wasted and trust is lost. And a presence that was built once and never maintained, so that a searcher in any month after the first finds an agent that looks as if it stopped paying attention. Avoid those, keep the profile accurate, the reviews honest, the pages plain, and the routine steady, and the agent will be easy to find, easy to trust, and easy to contact — which is all that local SEO for a real estate agent can honestly promise, and all that a prospective client, moving and deciding quickly, is asking for.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does local SEO take for a real estate agent?

There is no fixed timescale, and no honest answer can promise one. The basics — a complete and accurate profile, correct details everywhere, a website that answers the questions buyers and sellers ask, and pages for the areas and property types the agent serves — can be put in place in a few weeks, and the effect of those basics is often visible quickly, because many agents have never done them properly. The deeper work — a steady flow of reviews, helpful area and buying-and-selling pages, and a record of answering enquiries promptly, explaining the process plainly, and keeping clients informed — builds over months, and it is that record that keeps an agent visible over time. Judge the work by the enquiries and viewings it brings, over several months, rather than by a single week's position.

What should a real estate agent put on their Google Business Profile?

The exact agency or agent name, the correct phone number, the office address as it really is, the areas served, the opening hours as they really are, the services offered — sales, lettings, valuations, and property management, where the agent genuinely offers them — and real photographs of the office, the team, and the area. Add the areas and property types buyers and sellers search for, using the words they use, not only the formal terms. Keep the hours, the areas and the services up to date, answer the questions prospective clients ask on the profile, and post a short update at least once a week, so the profile looks like an agent that is working, active and paying attention.

How can a real estate agent get more Google reviews?

Ask at the right moment, in person, and make it easy — and check the advertising and conduct rules that apply to the agent before starting, because those rules vary and this guide does not state them. The right moment is when a sale or letting has completed well and the client is satisfied: a short, honest request from the agent who handled the matter, with a link the client can use on their phone, will bring in more reviews than any message sent weeks later. Never offer a reward for a review, never write a review for a client, and never ask only the happiest clients while avoiding the rest. Reply to every review, thank the client without discussing their property or their circumstances, and answer a critical review calmly, briefly, and without sharing any client details.

Does a real estate agent need a page for every area they serve?

An agent needs a page for every area they want to be found for, written for the buyer or seller in that area, because a single areas page that mentions every neighbourhood in a sentence will not answer any client's questions properly. An area page should say, honestly, what the agent does there: the kinds of property, the buyers and sellers served, and how a client from that area reaches the agent. Start with the areas the agent most wants to grow, write those pages first, and add the rest over time. One page that answers a client's questions for one area will serve the agent far better than one long page that mentions every area briefly.
